# Crichton's Prophecy (LinkedIn cut)

How *Disclosure* argued that taking the owner off a fragile product breaks the cycle — and why leadership still closes the room

> **For LinkedIn / professional networks.** Short cut of the longer essay. Focus: Crichton’s novel and 1994 interviews, product ownership, and Agile as a **goal**. The full century-map version lives on [paradigmthreat.net](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/crichtons-prophecy.md).

In **1994**, Michael Crichton published *Disclosure*. DigiCom is trying to ship a hard product under merger pressure. The person who owns manufacturing gets pulled into an accusation fight. Lawyers and office politics take over. The drive starts to fail.

Crichton saw that pattern early. A perfect defense helps. It rarely finishes the fight. In the book, the only thing that actually works is leadership: the owner goes back to the work, pulls the team together, finds what broke, and shows the room the fix. After that, the accusations stop mattering next to the product.

This is **not** an attack on Agile, Scrum, or the ideals people share under those names. No system ships without flaws — **leadership included**. Agile and Scrum are no exception. I believe strongly that Agile is a **goal** rather than a method. When a team actually reaches that goal, it can look like a utopian ideal, including thinning classic boss-flaws. The line I draw is blind faith that the method fixes everything — even when communication and organization have already broken down — when a short stretch of clear ownership would do the job.

## What Crichton said in public

He did not leave this only inside the novel. On Charlie Rose (**January 14, 1994**), he said sexual harassment is about **power** — a boss abusing a subordinate — and that putting a different sex in the chair does not magically rewrite a hierarchy. Tone might change. The machine stays.

In the afterword and on [his site](https://michaelcrichton.com/works/disclosure/), he said the story grew from a real governance headache a lawyer told him in **1987**: two executives, former lovers, competed for one job; each accused the other. He flipped the usual stereotype so readers could see what ordinary rhetoric hides — not a claim that every real case looks like DigiCom.

Around the same launch he drew a line between **egalitarian** feminism (equal opportunity and pay) and what he called **protectionist** feminism (special shields beyond equal treatment). Equality, he said, is measurable. Carve-outs get muddy.

## Other books that wanted a real decision-maker

In those same years, Lee Iacocca’s *Iacocca* said big decisions are made by **individuals**, not committees. Tom Peters and Robert Waterman’s *In Search of Excellence* told managers to act instead of drowning in analysis. *Disclosure* puts that instinct on a manufacturing floor: when the owner is deleted from the product, the fragile cycle can die.

## What showed up in software culture later

A year after *Disclosure*, Scrum got its public name. In **2001**, seventeen people at Snowbird published the [Manifesto for Agile Software Development](https://agilemanifesto.org/). The Japanese sources the rugby “scrum” idea borrowed from still kept strong leaders who owned the product. American software culture often treated hierarchy inside the team as the problem, while platform gardens stayed closed with real agreements and defaults.

That history matters. It does not mean the **goal** of Agile is worthless. It means ownership and empowerment are not opposites — and when communication has already collapsed, renaming the room does not replace a named owner who can decide and ship.

## The simple lesson

If you pull the person who owns a fragile product into a fight for their job, the cycle can break down. Process, counsel, and optics are useful. They are not a substitute for someone who can gather the team, fix what is broken, and show the room the answer.

Crichton stayed close to early industry trends. He built a story about a problem that keeps repeating: products need captains. That is the prophecy worth keeping in view.
