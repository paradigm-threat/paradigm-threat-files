# Crichton's Prophecy

How *Disclosure* warned that taking the boss off the product would break it — and what showed up later

> **TL;DR:** *Disclosure* (1994) stages a product captain deleted by accusation and untitled power — only doing the job ends the theater. Inside the novel, Crichton also warns that accusation climate can **destroy a family man at home** even after he proves innocence (the Masters story). DigiCom’s Twinkle CD-ROM fight names Sony and Philips the same decade Nintendo’s SNES CD deal blew up with those same houses. The century map under that office is communism as rear force, OSS/CIA next waves, Moore’s Law as paced hardware, and Agile/Scrum as software communism that deletes captains while gardens enclose — not only suppress at work, **eliminate** at work and at home.

![Crichton's Prophecy — hero](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/crichtons-prophecy.png)

In **1994**, Michael Crichton published *Disclosure*. The story is about a company shipping a hard product under pressure. Someone in charge gets hit with an accusation. Lawyers and office politics take over. The product starts to fail. Crichton saw that pattern early. Proving you are innocent is not enough. Getting a lucky recording is not enough. The only thing that actually works is leadership: the person goes back to work, pulls his people together, fixes what is broken, and shows the room the answer. After that, the accusations stop mattering. He also staged a harder warning: in a climate that treats the mention of abuse as already proven, a family man can lose wife, children, friends, and career **even after the dates make the charge impossible**. Software companies later wrote rules that made bosses look illegitimate inside the team. People with no clear job title still ran mergers and careers from behind the scenes. Writers named the bad habits without needing everyone to agree that communism was even real. The same force had been changing costumes for a century. DigiCom was one office in a longer fight — delete the captain at work, and delete the father at home.

## What happens in *Disclosure*

DigiCom is trying to ship a CD-ROM drive called Twinkle from a plant in Malaysia. A big merger with Conley-White is hanging over everyone. Tom Sanders runs manufacturing for that drive. Meredith Johnson used to sleep with him. She gets the promotion he expected. She harasses him, then turns around and falsely accuses him. Bob Garvin is the company founder. Phil Blackburn is the general counsel — the top lawyer. While Tom is stuck defending his name, the drive falls apart.

The product twist is Meredith’s own. She had ordered the Malaysia quality cuts and sold them as the cost-saving measure that helped her win the job and keep the merger on track. That same “savings” program was the sabotage that wrecked Twinkle and set Sanders up to look incompetent — and she put it in motion **before** she sexually harassed him. Once that order of events lands, she reads more like a cartoon villain with deep psychological scars: the glass-ceiling climb, the cost theater, and the harassment charge are all one ambitious stroke.

At the top of the company, people who do not even have the right title on the door are already calling a lot of the shots. Tom gets one lucky break. He leaves a **cell phone** line open during the harassment. The call records onto an answering machine at a lawyer’s office. The film has a similar recording beat. The tape helps, and it still does not finish the fight. A settlement on the harassment charge does not save him either. Counsel still tries to push him out over the product. The next day Tom walks into a meeting and explains what went wrong with Twinkle and how to fix it. Meredith’s story stops landing. He is back on the work. Stephanie Kaplan ends up with the vice-president job. 

Crichton skipped real company names and built DigiCom as fiction that still tracks how modern software and hardware shops fight inside: if you drag the person who owns the product into a fight for his job, a fragile project can die.

## The simple lesson

If you falsly accuse the person in charge of a product of misconduct, the whole cycle can break down. In that era, having authority over other people is treated like a character flaw — something to watch, punish, and manage. A perfect tape and a good defense are useful. You will rarely get that tape in real life. Even when you do, it may not end the career attack.

In the book, Tom figures out the only way through. He does his job. He gathers his team. He solves the plant problem. The next day he presents the fix. Suddenly the accusations look small next to the work. Leadership is what closes it.

## The family kill — Crichton’s harder warning

Midway through the novel, after a newspaper hit piece paints Tom as a pig, the narration drops a cautionary story that had been circulating among senior men in Seattle: **John Masters**, a marketing manager at another firm, married twenty-five years, two daughters. A child psychologist plants abuse suspicion. The wife throws him out. The state investigates. Friends and colleagues treat him as guilty on contact. Masters digs up canceled checks and calendars and proves he was in Germany the summer the charge places him at home — he never even saw his daughter that summer. The state drops the case. His company reinstates him. His wife and younger daughter still insist abuse happened (wrong date, same claim). Divorce follows. He never sees the younger daughter again. Career cloud, heart attack, drinking, abandoned by the room. His question: what did I do wrong?

The charge does not need proof. The mention is enough. Clearing the calendar does not bring the family back. Crichton is warning that this climate grows until any family man can lose home, children, and standing at the mere mention of harassment or abuse — no reasoning to start it, no proof to finish it.

That is the nature of the force under investigation here. Communism as rear influence does not only **suppress** leaders on the product floor. It reaches into the household. It does not only demote. It **eliminates**. 

## What Crichton said in public

He did not leave this only inside the novel. When *Disclosure* came out in early **1994**, he went on TV and into magazines and explained what he thought he was trying to say.

Charlie Rose hosted a long-running late-night interview show on public television. Guests sat at a table and talked for a stretch — writers, politicians, scientists. On **January 14, 1994**, [Crichton came on to talk about *Disclosure*](https://charlierose.com/videos/18483). He said sexual harassment is about **power** — a boss abusing a subordinate. He said he did not expect women who move into power to behave in some totally different species of way from men. Some people in charge abuse people under them. That is the pattern. He also talked about **hierarchy** inside big companies: once you are inside a chain of command, the structure itself pushes hard on how you behave. You do not have endless freedom. The idea that putting a different sex in the chair will magically rewrite the whole machine, he said, was going to prove to be a fantasy. Tone might change. The machine stays.

**The afterword and his own notes.** At the end of the book he added a short author’s note. He said the story was based on a real episode a lawyer told him in **1987**: two executives, former lovers, competed for the same job; they met privately; the next day each accused the other of harassment; the company did not know whom to fire. The lawyer framed it as a governance headache. Crichton thought it was a better story than that. He flipped the **usual stereotype** — woman as powerful accuser, man as subordinate — not a real-life verdict that the man was to blame. He said that was not meant to deny that most real harassment claims are women accusing men. The flip was a way to see what ordinary rhetoric usually hides. He compared the story to a Rorschach inkblot: what you see in it says a lot about you. On [his site](https://michaelcrichton.com/works/disclosure/) he later wrote that feminist columnists often attacked the book without reading it, while working businesswomen told him they liked it because it named the unscrupulous corporate climber they could not safely criticize in public.

**Egalitarian vs protectionist feminism.** In a *Time* interview around the same launch (*“Pop Fiction’s Prime Provocateur,”* January 1994) and in a *Los Angeles Times* interview the same month, he drew a hard line. He said he believed in equality of opportunity and pay — what he called **egalitarian** feminism. He rejected what he called **protectionist** feminism: the idea that women need special shields and special rules beyond equal treatment. Equality, he said, is clear and measurable. Protectionism is muddy. Once you start carving exceptions, free speech and fairness get blurry. That is the public politics he put next to DigiCom: he was arguing about power and special protection, not only about bedroom manners.

Chrichton's prophecy is simple: **protectionist feminism would not end up benefiting women.** What arrived looks a lot like that warning. Special-protection politics kept expanding — including toward LGBTQIA claims — until plenty of critics, including feminists and minority advocates, said women and other protected classes were being pushed aside while the new carve-outs took the mic. Crichton named the muddiness of protectionism in **1994**. The later fight over who gets the shield is the bill coming due.

**How he talked about the harassment debate itself.** In a USA Weekly profile the week the book landed, he complained that public talk about harassment sounded like children describing sex they had never had. Adults know messy feelings — overstepping, regret, being talked into something, walking away and wishing you hadn’t. Laws and op-eds, he said, often pretend none of that exists. He wanted the novel to feel like the real office, not a clean pamphlet.

***Rising Sun* and Japan.** Two years earlier he had published *Rising Sun* (1992), a thriller that also worked as a warning about U.S.–Japan business competition. In interviews and after-matter around that book he said Japan rebuilt for **quality** after the war while America drifted toward **cost** and short-term stock pressure. Japanese firms were competitors, not saviors. America had gotten soft and complacent. That is the public scoreboard of the early 1990s: compete with Japan, fix quality, stop kidding yourself. *Disclosure* puts **Sony and Philips** in the acquirer’s mouth as the electronics rivals DigiCom has to beat — the same decade’s hardware anxiety, now attached to a CD-ROM drive and a merger.

**The Nintendo CD-ROM parallel.** In **1988**, Nintendo and **Sony** began a CD-ROM add-on for the Super Nintendo — then at CES **1991**, Nintendo publicly flipped to **Philips** the day after Sony announced the deal. The rupture helped birth the PlayStation and cost Kyoto its CD future in a format war and partner betrayal. In the novel, DigiCom’s Twinkle is a CD-ROM drive, and the merger people name **Sony** and **Philips** as the rivals DigiCom has to beat. Nintendo kept real people in charge of the product at home; the games other companies remade from that material often did not — the same story as Japanese shops that kept bosses and American Scrum that wrote them out ([The Nintendo–Sony Breakup](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/nintendo-sony-breakup.md)).

**The WIRED essay that cuts the other way.** In April **1993**, in WIRED’s essay [*Mediasaurus*](https://www.wired.com/1993/04/mediasaurus/), Crichton told news companies they needed the same painful rebuild other U.S. firms had already tried: flatten the hierarchy, push information up from the bottom, empower workers, chase quality instead of glitz. He could praise a quality-driven flatten in media. In *Disclosure* he staged a company where sex, lawyers, and hallway power delete the product captain while the public story is Japan and the glass ceiling.

**What this public set adds up to.** He said out loud that harassment is power. Hierarchy shapes behavior. Special protection is a trap. Japan competition and quality-for-cost are real. Working climbers abuse process. Inside the novel he staged the cautionary: innocence on the calendar does not restore a destroyed career or family. He named the front-facing problems that still matter today: authority treated as a scandal, process and counsel over the product, untitled power at the top, accusation climate that can eliminate the household, Japan talk as the scoreboard while the real fight is inside the building.

## Other books that argued for a real decision-maker

In those same years, other famous business books wanted a person who decides instead of a fog of committees.

Lee Iacocca’s *Iacocca* (1984), written with William Novak, says the big decisions in a company are made by **individuals**, not by committees. He listened to everyone up to the decision. Then he became the boss: okay, I heard you; here is what we are going to do. Committees are fine for sharing knowledge. When committees replace the person who decides, the work gets worse. Japan and the industrial base were already on people’s minds — the same air as *Rising Sun*.

Tom Peters and Robert Waterman’s *In Search of Excellence* (1982) told managers to act instead of drowning in analysis and committee reports. Crichton’s *Rising Sun* put the Japan fight and the quality-for-cost fight into a mass novel. *Disclosure* looks like an HR thriller until you set it against the **1900s through the Cold War**: public bosses get knocked down while the people with no honest title keep running things.

## The word he never said

Communism sits in the **rear** — behind the scenes. It is the background force and influence. The threats people see out front often do not look communist at all. Radical Islam is one example of a front that is not a Party project. The front sells freedom, protection, and “no more bosses.” What arrives in the back is more bureaucracy, more enclosure, and fewer people who can lead in public.

In the U.S.A. many leaders simply believed that communism did not exist at home — the same way a lot of people later treated radical Islam as something that could not really be a seriou threat. In my lifetime, if you said the C-word, you get called a McCarthyite before anyone listens. School re-inforced this brick in the wall. We all got the message. So careful writers named the bad habits instead: protection rackets dressed as justice, PC language, treating hierarchy itself as a scandal, power with no title, Japan panic as cover. That is the set of problems he put on the table. He did not need to wreck his own standing by announcing that communism was “behind” feminism or DigiCom’s lawyers. The goal was to get people to see the specific force and resist it. He did not have to win a world-saving argument about the whole system.

He fought the front-facing problem in front of him. That was enough. He is not rewritten here as someone who believed communism stood behind feminism. The twin pattern — sell liberation, deliver enclosure — is already walked through in [THIS IS AMERICA percentage ads](https://paradigmthreat.net/governance/usury/communism/investigations/this-is-america-percentage-ads-democratic-bait-switch-investigation.md).

## Communism did not only happen in Russia

Soft history likes a simple map: communism equals the Soviet Union, then China, then a few posters. That map is wrong on purpose. In the **early twentieth century**, revolutionary and collectivist politics were a **global** pressure. Russia’s **1917** revolution was the loudest success story. It was not the only storm.

After World War I, workers’ councils, party cells, and revolutionary parties showed up across Europe and beyond. Germany had its **1918–19** upheavals and the short-lived Bavarian soviet experiment. Hungary had a **1919** soviet republic. Italy’s “Red Biennium” put factories and streets under radical pressure before Mussolini’s counter-move. China had the **Chinese Communist Party** founded in **1921** and decades of civil war. Colonial and semi-colonial countries got their own Marxist and Leninist imports. The point is not a checklist of every country that flew a red flag. The point is the **pattern**: collectivist politics that attack the **named captain** — the owner, the officer, the product leader, the national tip — and replace him with process, party, committee, or “the people,” while **untitled** machines keep running.

## Fronts and costumes

**Fascism** in Italy and **National Socialism** in Germany were sold as nationalist, anti-Bolshevik, and anti-liberal packages. They looked like the opposite of Moscow. On this read they still belong in the **same family of machines**: mass process over the individual tip, party and apparatus over honest public ownership of outcomes, theater of the nation while **untitled** power and international finance networks do the real routing. American forms showed up too — corporate cartels, managed “relief,” red-scare and anti-red-scare both used as clubs, later managerial ideologies that treat a boss as a moral defect. Soft history wants fascism and communism as eternal enemies who cancel each other out. The useful question is who **kept winning** when nationalist authoritarian tips tried to hold a country against a **globalist fascist** machine.

Again and again, the **nationalist authoritarian** side — the side that still wants a **named** tip responsible for a people or a product — **lost ground** to the globalist costume. Communism was the **driving rear force** behind that pressure: not always the Party card on the desk, often the **anti-captain** grammar wearing whatever brand sold that year. Sell liberation. Sell protection. Sell “no more bosses.” Deliver enclosure. Deliver counsel. Deliver a room that cannot decide. DigiCom’s hallway is that grammar in a Seattle office. The percentage-ad twin — democratic bait, corporatist catch — is the same double in civic clothes.

**The Big Irony**: Anti-leadership sales pitch still needs leaders - to blame for everything that goes wrong. A country, a party, or a company cannot run on posters alone. Communism needs a tip as much as nationalism does. The difference is the tip’s job. Under communist influence the public face is often — and for the machine, needs to be — a **puppet**: someone the shadows can keep, replace, and blame when public government ventures **fail to benefit the people**. A real captain who owns outcomes and cannot be discarded defeats the purpose. Soft history says “no leaders.” The working design is **disposable leaders** and **untitled power** behind them.

Soft history personifies communism as **Stalin**. The fight inside the USSR was different. In the **1920s** Stalin’s camp pushed **[socialism in one country](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism_in_One_Country)** — build and hold the Soviet state first — against Trotsky’s **permanent revolution**, which treated world export of the revolution as the real job. That is a named tip fighting for a country against an internationalist machine, still sitting inside a communist operating system he did not invent.

## World War II — the failure to destroy Russia

World War II is usually told as Allies versus Axis, democracy versus fascism, good versus evil. Those frames are not empty. They are also incomplete.

On this read, one of the war’s load-bearing outcomes is that **communism failed to destroy Russia**. The Soviet Union took catastrophic losses. It also **held**. The Axis fronts in **Italy**, **Japan**, and **Germany** went down. Whatever mix of ideology, empire, and industrial murder those regimes carried, their public costumes burned. Russia’s spine — including what soft history refuses to call **nationalist authoritarianism** at the tip when it suits the tip — remained one of the few large challenges to a **deep-state globalist communism** vision of how the world should be run.

That failure mattered. If the goal was to wipe out the last big rival tips and leave only managed process, the war did **not** finish the job. The enemy had to learn. Soft history celebrates 1945 as closure. For the people designing the next wave, 1945 was a **lesson plan**: Russia’s challenge survived; the Axis costumes did not; the next communism waves would have to be smarter, quieter, and better at deleting captains **inside** the West rather than only bombing them from outside.

## The enemy within — OSS and the CIA

After the war, American covert capacity did not go home and dissolve into ordinary civilian life. The **Office of Strategic Services** — **OSS** — had been built in **1942** for wartime espionage, sabotage, and liaison work. From **1944** it ran **[Project SAFEHAVEN](https://www.cia.gov/resources/csi/static/oss-project-safehaven.pdf)** — the U.S. hunt for Nazi gold and other assets moving into neutral countries. The looted-gold paper trail is public ([Eizenstat / State briefings](https://1997-2001.state.gov/www/policy_remarks/1998/980602_eizenstat_nazigld.html)). That hunt capitalized the postwar intel networks that reappeared as the **Central Intelligence Agency** in **1947** under the National Security Act. Personnel, methods, networks, and appetites carried forward. Full braid: [CIA investigation](https://paradigmthreat.net/governance/war/investigations/cia-investigation.md).

The claim here is simple enough for a kitchen table. Having failed to **contain and destroy** Russia’s nationalist-authoritarian challenge in the war’s full sense, the next communism waves were set in motion from **inside** Western institutions. Covert action, asset capture, regime games, information cages, and later cultural and labor operating systems would do what tanks did not finish. The tactics were **lessons learned**: do not only fight the rival tip on a battlefield; hollow out the places that still produce captains — universities, corporations, software shops, Hollywood grammars, finance.

America itself was in **deep trouble** for a long stretch. The **Great Depression** was not only a weather pattern of markets. It was a managed wreck that consolidated power while people starved and information stayed one-way — the story told in [The Great Depression Was Planned](https://paradigmthreat.net/governance/war/the-great-depression-was-planned.md). Overseas wars kept the country bleeding and distracted while the domestic tip stayed soft. For decades it was fair to say America was **not** the main challenge to the globalist-communism vision. Russia was. China after Mao’s succession fights became another durable managed form. America is now challenging that vision too. That is new relative to the long sick stretch. It does not erase how bad the Depression and the war habits were.

## Software communism and a paced hardware revolution

![Agile / Scrum versus Leadership — investigation cover](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/investigations/agile-scrum-leadership-containment-investigation.png)

By the **1990s**, the fight had a new floor: **software**. Hardware was leaping — chips, drives, networks, consumer machines. A real hardware revolution can throw up **surprises**: new companies, new captains, new stacks that do not ask permission. Soft history says the only limit was physics. **Moore’s Law** — Gordon Moore’s **1965** observation that transistor counts on chips tended to double on a predictable schedule — got sold to the public as a law of nature. Plan your product roadmaps around the curve. Do not expect miracles. Wait your turn.

That sales pitch did useful work for enclosure. If everyone believes the pace is **physics**, nobody asks whether faster paths were **suppressed**, starved of funding, or locked behind patents and vendor gardens. Analog shortcuts, open stacks, and rival captains become “unrealistic.” The curve becomes a **self-imposed** schedule — suppressed science dressed as humility. Related reads: [Quantum Leap (of faith)](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/quantum-leap-of-faith.md) and [The Internet That Should Have Been](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/the-internet-that-should-have-been.md).

On top of that paced hardware story, the office got a **software communism** install. Make **command illegitimate** inside the team. Sell trust, empowerment, and “no hierarchies.” Ship ticket queues, certificate industries, and Product Owners who cannot settle technical fights. Keep the **platform gardens** — Microsoft, Apple, Google — enclosed with real agreements and defaults while labor inside the garden stops producing captains. That is the same rear force in a hoodie: liberation talk up front, enclosure and untitled power in back.

*Disclosure* (**1994**) sits at the front of that decade’s controversy. A year later Scrum got its public name. A few years after that, Snowbird locked the brand. Crichton did not write the Scrum Guide. He wrote the antibody: when accusation and counsel delete the product captain, **only leadership** ends the theater.

## What showed up after *Disclosure*

In **1995**, a year after *Disclosure*, Scrum got its public name at a software conference (OOPSLA). Jeff Sutherland and Ken Schwaber had been writing and running the method in the early 1990s. Sutherland later said Scrum was “**specifically designed**” against named “laws” of software uncertainty — a **prescriptive** invent, not a farm custom that grew by itself in every shop.

In **February 2001**, seventeen people met at a ski lodge in Snowbird, Utah, and published a real document called the **Manifesto for Agile Software Development** — still online at [agilemanifesto.org](https://agilemanifesto.org/). That is not a nickname somebody invented later. That is the official title. One of the writers, Martin Fowler, said they called it a manifesto because it was a “call to arms” and a statement of what they believed. The group’s own history even jokes that they were “organizational anarchists.”

Once you hear **Agile Manifesto** next to the **Communist Manifesto** — Marx and Engels, **1848**, the famous political declaration — the name match is hard to un-hear. Same English word for a loud public creed. Later critics and jokes have pointed it out a lot: Agile is like communism, sounds good on paper, and when it fails the true believers say it was not *real* Agile. Fowler said they wanted a call to arms. They chose a revolutionary title, not a quiet process memo.

American software culture then said teams should have **no hierarchies**. Someone called the Product Owner lines up the work list. The fights that actually stall shipping are usually technical — how the machine is built. The Product Owner often does not know that machine well enough to settle those fights. The Japanese companies the idea was borrowed from still kept strong leaders who owned the product. Takeuchi and Nonaka’s **1986** Harvard Business Review paper described rugby “scrum” teams under **subtle control**: top management set the walls, hand-picked people, and would add a conservative member if the room went radical. Toyota’s **shusa** and Honda’s large-project leaders still **signed** the product. America took the rugby word and dropped the captain.

The same decades that baptized Agile also locked the **enclosure tank**. **1997**: Microsoft and Apple’s Technology Agreement — money, Mac Office, Internet Explorer as Mac default, patent peace — later treated partly as coercion in *U.S. v. Microsoft*. **1998–2001**: the Microsoft antitrust fight and consent decree. **2005** onward: Silicon Valley no-poach lists among Apple, Google, and others. Later: huge default-search payments from Google to Apple. Years later, Apple, Google, and Microsoft products still fight each other at the edges. One brand at a time can feel fine. Mix them and documents break, calendars disagree, messages look wrong. When the rules treat a clear boss as the problem, people spend whole careers moving tickets instead of breaking those walls.

That is the controversy of the software 1990s and 2000s in plain clothes: a hardware revolution paced like physics, a labor religion that deletes captains, and gardens that stay closed. *Disclosure* warned what happens when the captain is deleted. Agile wrote the deletion into the employee handbook. The longer write-up with dates and quotes is linked under Where next.

## Same fight, office version

This is bigger than one company’s HR file. Hollywood stories already sort into two habits. One habit keeps a named person in charge, checks the sabotage, and ends the drama by finishing the work. The other habit sells process, public image, accusations, and power held by people with no honest title. Those habits get called NSA-aligned and CIA/globalist — sorting labels for stories and workplaces.

*Disclosure* came out in **1994**, the same year as *Stargate*, just before *Independence Day*, and next to Crichton’s *Congo*. On the Hollywood catalog it sits on the **NSA-aligned** list as a corporate captain antibody: do the job, audit the sabotage, keep the named lead. *Stargate* the same year is the contrast costume — ancient-mystery misdirection, sketchy plot, ridicule training. Scrum starts growing in that same stretch of years. The office is one lane of the same fight. ## After the Soviet Union — leaders fall, shadows stay

When the USSR collapsed, soft history celebrated the end of history. The rear force did not end. Chinese communism, and other governments that survived after Moscow’s public fall, look a lot like DigiCom and the no-hierarchies office at **country** size. Public leaders rise and get knocked down. People in the shadows keep running things. The country does not need a clear public captain who owns the outcome the way Tom owns Twinkle. Faces on posters are disposable. Continuity lives in apparatus, party organs, and informal networks.

Some academic papers call China’s fast rule-making on tech and AI **agile governance** or **responsive authoritarianism**. They mean government agencies that change regulations quickly — a different job from the software method called Scrum. Baiyang Xiao’s paper [“Agile and iterative governance: China’s regulatory response to AI”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212473X25000562) is one example of that academic language. Same English word. The useful parallel here is simpler: faces on posters come and go; the people without the public title keep the machine running. That is the century’s rhyme written on a national scale — the same rhyme *Disclosure* put on a manufacturing floor in 1994.

## Framing and limits

[Prisca sapientia](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/PRISCA_SAPIENTIA.md) — older pattern knowledge kept where soft history softens it — fits this page.
