# Crichton's Prophecy

How *Disclosure* warned that taking the boss off the product would break it — and what showed up later

![Agile / Scrum versus Leadership — investigation cover reused for Crichton's Prophecy](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/investigations/agile-scrum-leadership-containment-investigation.png)

In **1994**, Michael Crichton published *Disclosure*. The story is about a company shipping a hard product under pressure. Someone in charge gets hit with an accusation. Lawyers and office politics take over. The product starts to fail. Crichton saw that pattern early. Proving you are innocent is not enough. Getting a lucky recording is not enough. The only thing that actually works is leadership: the person goes back to work, pulls his people together, fixes what is broken, and shows the room the answer. After that, the accusations stop mattering. Software companies later wrote rules that made bosses look illegitimate inside the team. People with no clear job title still ran mergers and careers from behind the scenes. Writers named the bad habits without needing everyone to agree that communism was even real.

## What happens in *Disclosure*

DigiCom is trying to ship a CD-ROM drive called Twinkle from a plant in Malaysia. A big merger with Conley-White is hanging over everyone. Tom Sanders runs manufacturing for that drive. Meredith Johnson used to sleep with him. She gets the promotion he expected. She harasses him, then turns around and falsely accuses him. Bob Garvin is the company founder. Phil Blackburn is the general counsel — the top lawyer. People at the plant are cutting corners on purpose and wrecking quality. While Tom is stuck defending his name, the drive falls apart.

Tom gets one lucky break. He leaves a **cell phone** line open. The call records onto an answering machine at a lawyer’s office. That is a sound recording, not a video. The movie version is closer to a cellphone message on voicemail. Either way, the tape helps, and it still does not finish the fight. A settlement on the harassment charge does not save him either. Counsel still tries to push him out over the product. The next day Tom walks into a meeting and explains what went wrong with Twinkle and how to fix it. Meredith’s story stops landing. He is back on the work. Stephanie Kaplan ends up with the vice-president job. At the top of the company, people who do not even have the right title on the door are already calling a lot of the shots — lawyers, climbers, friends in the hall.

Crichton never says Microsoft, Apple, Google, Agile, or Scrum. He still understood the basic problem: if you drag the person who owns the product into a fight for his job, a fragile project can die.

## The simple lesson

If you accuse the person in charge of the product, the whole cycle can stop. In that era, having authority over other people is treated like a character flaw — something to watch, punish, and manage. A perfect tape and a good defense are useful. You will rarely get that tape in real life. Even when you do, it may not end the career attack.

In the book, Tom figures out the only way through. He does his job. He gathers his team. He solves the plant problem. The next day he presents the fix. Suddenly the accusations look small next to the work. Leadership is what closes it.

## What Crichton said in public

He talked about this outside the novel too. In the *Disclosure* afterword and on Charlie Rose he said harassment was really about power and hierarchy, not only manners. He criticized a style of feminism that was about protection and privilege. He said hierarchy shapes how people behave. In the *Rising Sun* afterword, and in the Japan talk of that decade, he treated Japanese companies as real competitors and said America had sold quality for cheap cost. In a 1993 WIRED article he even praised companies that flattened their org chart to chase quality in media. So he was not a cartoon opponent of every flatten. He could like a quality push in one place and still write DigiCom in another: real captains get pushed aside while sex, lawyers, and hallway power decide the merger, and the public story is Japan and the glass ceiling.

## Other books that argued for a real decision-maker

In those same years, other famous business books wanted a person who decides instead of a fog of committees.

Lee Iacocca’s *Iacocca* (1984), written with William Novak, says the big decisions in a company are made by **individuals**, not by committees. He listened to everyone up to the decision. Then he became the boss: okay, I heard you; here is what we are going to do. Committees are fine for sharing knowledge. When committees replace the person who decides, the work gets worse. Japan and the industrial base were already on people’s minds — the same air as *Rising Sun*.

Tom Peters and Robert Waterman’s *In Search of Excellence* (1982) told managers to act instead of drowning in analysis and committee reports. They were writing about how companies run. They were not writing about communism or Scrum. Crichton’s *Rising Sun* (1992) put the Japan fight and the quality-for-cost fight into a mass novel. None of these writers named Agile. They still wanted someone who would make the call.

## What showed up after *Disclosure*

In **1995**, a year after *Disclosure*, Scrum got its public name at a software conference. In **2001**, seventeen people at Snowbird wrote the Agile Manifesto. American software culture then said teams should have **no hierarchies**. Someone called the Product Owner lines up the work list. The fights that actually stall shipping are usually technical — how the machine is built. The Product Owner often does not know that machine well enough to settle those fights. The Japanese companies the idea was borrowed from still kept strong leaders who owned the product. America took the rugby word “scrum” and dropped that part.

Years later, Apple, Google, and Microsoft products still fight each other at the edges. One brand at a time can feel fine. Mix them and documents break, calendars disagree, messages look wrong. When the rules treat a clear boss as the problem, people spend whole careers moving tickets instead of breaking those walls. The longer write-up with dates and quotes is linked under Where next. This page is the plain warning.

## The word he never said

Communism sits in the **rear** — behind the scenes. It is the background force and influence. The threats people see out front often do not look communist at all. Radical Islam is one example of a front that is not a Party project. The front sells freedom, protection, and “no more bosses.” What arrives in the back is more bureaucracy, more enclosure, and fewer people who can lead in public.

Crichton never said “communism.” That fits the country he was writing in. A lot of people treated communism like it barely existed — the same way a lot of people later treated radical Islam as something that could not really be the issue. Say the C-word and you get called a McCarthyite before anyone listens. So careful writers named the bad habits instead: protection rackets dressed as justice, PC language, treating hierarchy itself as a scandal, power with no title, Japan panic as cover. That is the set of problems he put on the table. He did not need to wreck his own standing by announcing that communism was “behind” feminism or DigiCom’s lawyers. The goal was to get people to see the specific force and resist it. He did not have to win a world-saving argument about the whole system.

He fought the front-facing problem in front of him. That was enough. He is not rewritten here as someone who believed communism stood behind feminism. The twin pattern — sell liberation, deliver enclosure — is already walked through in [THIS IS AMERICA percentage ads](https://paradigmthreat.net/governance/usury/communism/investigations/this-is-america-percentage-ads-democratic-bait-switch-investigation.md).

## Same fight, office version

This is bigger than one company’s HR file. Hollywood stories already sort into two habits. One habit keeps a named person in charge, checks the sabotage, and ends the drama by finishing the work. The other habit sells process, public image, accusations, and power held by people with no honest title. Those habits get called NSA-aligned and CIA/globalist. That is a sorting label for stories and workplaces. Nobody is claiming DigiCom or Snowbird got a memo from an agency.

*Disclosure* came out in **1994**, the same year as *Stargate*, just before *Independence Day*, and next to Crichton’s *Congo*. Scrum starts growing in that same stretch of years. The office is one lane of the same fight. [Everything Burns](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/hollywood/everything-burns.md) covers the Pacific movie lane. This page stays on the product floor.

## After the Soviet Union — leaders fall, shadows stay

Chinese communism, and other governments that survived after the USSR collapsed, look a lot like this pattern at country size. Public leaders rise and get knocked down. People in the shadows keep running things. The country does not need a clear public captain who owns the outcome the way Tom owns Twinkle. That is DigiCom’s top floor, and the software office that says “no bosses,” written large enough to run a nation.

Some academic papers call China’s fast rule-making on tech and AI **agile governance** or **responsive authoritarianism**. They mean government agencies that change regulations quickly. They do **not** mean the software method called Scrum. Baiyang Xiao’s paper [“Agile and iterative governance: China’s regulatory response to AI”](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212473X25000562) is one example of that academic language. Same English word. Totally different job. A journal saying “agile” does not mean Beijing adopted a software Scrum Guide. The useful parallel is simpler: faces on posters come and go; the people without the public title keep the machine running.

## Framing and limits

[Prisca sapientia](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/PRISCA_SAPIENTIA.md) — older pattern knowledge kept where soft history softens it — fits this page. A novel is not a court transcript. The book and the film differ; this page follows the novel. Crichton did not name Agile, Scrum, Marx, or the Communist Party. Camp labels are a way to sort stories and workplaces, not leaked agency files. “Banned word” here means you get socially punished for saying it — not that a 1994 law banned the noun.

Crichton named the problems he could name in public. This page does not pretend he held the full “communism behind feminism” map. Iacocca and Peters argue for real decision-makers in their own books; they sit next to *Disclosure*, they are not the same book. The China / post-Soviet parallel is the same pattern at country size — leaders fall, shadows stay — without saying Beijing runs software Scrum. When academics say “agile governance,” they mean fast government rule-making, not the Scrum Guide.
