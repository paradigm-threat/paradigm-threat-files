## 1. The comment and the name

**King Alfred the Great**, on the Substack of [JFK Was Never Assassinated](/events/jfk/jfk-was-never-assassinated.md), about one day before this file opened:

> What. about Lady Di? the letter, the affair, the butler, the driver, the evil prince, the distraught princess...not one person has ever looked at this...its screams fake

**Lady Di** is **Diana Frances Spencer** (1 July 1961 – the death announced 31 August 1997). Before the wedding she was Lady Diana Spencer. After 29 July 1981 she was Princess of Wales. After the 28 August 1996 divorce the style in palace use was Diana, Princess of Wales. The press kept “Princess Diana” and “Lady Di.” **Diane** is a different spelling. The comment means Diana.

The cast he listed is the public soap, and each part has a name:

| the butler | Paul Burrell |

“Not one person has ever looked” is the sentence this file can answer. The answer is the size of the coverage, in [§1.1](#11-how-much-the-press-looked). “It screams fake” is the sentence it cannot close. That claim is why the file sits beside the JFK essay. It is not a finding that she lived.

This dossier is its own file, not a section of [British royalty as a controlled scheme](/governance/war/investigations/british-royalty-controlled-scheme-investigation.md). The royalty file keeps the Crown, the oustings, and the Russia war. Diana’s death is the one exit that came with a measured press record larger than the Kennedy assassination. That record is the thing King Alfred’s question has to meet.

## 1.1 How much the press looked

The comment was left under an essay about Kennedy. A cuttings agency had already compared the two deaths.

On **1 October 1997** the *Independent* reported Durrants Press Cuttings, which then monitored about **200,000** newspapers and magazines a year. Managing director Tony Law said no subject in the firm’s archive, back to **1880**, matched the coverage of Diana’s death, funeral, and the stories after. The Kennedy assassination and the Lennon shooting, he said, “pale into insignificance” in column inches. The nearest comparison was the Second World War, and even the outbreak, Dunkirk, and VE-Day did not reach the Diana coverage. Royal births, weddings, and deaths in that century were, in his words, only a small fraction ([Independent, 1 Oct 1997](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/media-diana-coverage-set-record-1233239.html)).

Durrants published a ranking, not an inch count. The number of columns is still unstated. The ranking is the fact in hand: in a British cuttings archive, her death outranked the event the comment was attached to.

**The 59 percent.** It is the weak half of that row. The *Independent* and the *Times* called the BARB release preliminary. The trade note gives the millions, then says the audience “represents 59% of the entire nation,” and does not show the division. The mid-1997 UK population was **58,314,200** ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/populationandmigration/populationestimates/timeseries/ukpop)). **31.3 million** is **53.7 percent** of that population. **31.8 million** is **54.5 percent**. **59 percent** of **58.3 million** would be **34.4 million**, which is not the audience they printed. No later BARB slip has turned up that withdraws the thirty-one million. What has been contested is the reading that the country watched as one. Silke Fürst, citing Robert Turnock’s *Interpreting Diana* (2000), pages 91 and 104, writes that audience research found only about half the British population watched parts of the funeral, not the whole nation ([*International Journal of Communication* 14, 2020](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/11036/3007/44299)). Half of the census population is the figure that matches the millions. Fifty-nine percent of “the entire nation” does not. The same *Independent* piece’s “three-quarters of British adults” is a second gloss in the same paragraph, with no adult count beside it. One channel’s audience was about **19 million**. The thirty-one million is BBC and ITV added.

**What the 59 percent was not.** It was not people who went out. The crowd on the route, in the BBC’s later summary, was “over a million” ([§1.1](#11-how-much-the-press-looked) table). A million is about **2 percent** of the **58.3 million**, and the route was in London. For most of the country the funeral was not walking distance.

The television number has a mechanical explanation beside popularity. Saturday **6 September** was cleared.

Turn on the main channels that morning and the funeral is what is on. The match, the shop, and the film are not available as the rival plan. The *Times* added that millions more were listening on radio in cars and at work, which is outside the television count. Popularity can fill a set that is already tuned to the only programme. It is not the only thing that was arranged. The arrangement was: stay in, both big channels, nothing else on the Saturday list until the service was over.

The press also said the volume had been too much, and then wrote the rules for the next one. On **18 September 1997** Lord Wakeham, chairman of the Press Complaints Commission, met **12** editors. The *Independent on Sunday* told its readers: “In our dealings with Diana, we behaved like children and we never had enough of her. The paparazzi were chasing her on our behalf because we did not know when to stop” ([Irish Times, 18 Sep 1997](https://www.irishtimes.com/news/move-towards-press-code-1.107417)). *Time*’s 2017 account of the code says that from **January 1998** long-lens pictures of people in private places without consent were unacceptable, and that an editor answered for pursuit pictures whether a staffer or a freelancer took them ([Time, 2017](https://time.com/4914324/princess-diana-anniversary-paparazzi-tabloid-media/)).

Almond, in that Nieman piece, added that she was not yet a saint but that he expected a rash of sightings, “like Elvis.” That is a reporter’s joke in **June 1998**, not a sighting. It sits next to reading 3 in [§6](#6-four-readings): the coverage was already large enough that a working journalist predicted the Elvis afterlife as the next product.

**Author.** The inches, the BARB millions, and the front pages a year later are the lament, counted. The share to repeat is about half the people in the country, not 59 percent. A story nobody looked at does not rewrite the editors’ code, sell an extra half-million broadsheets, and still lead eight of eleven dailies on the anniversary. The correct answer to King Alfred is that the looking is the largest part of the record. What the looking was for is [§8](#8-the-purpose-if-it-was-on-purpose): a princess removed, and a Crown left farther from the people.

## 1.3 How forced the telling was

**Author** (21 Nov 2026, later the same day):

> The way the history of the coverage is described is that this was probably the most publicized event in all of human history, and so widely and heavily published. Almost akin to that movie *V for Vendetta*, when the government starts hammering people with repetitive, nostalgic, pro-government propaganda. It seems that Britain is known for that. And I can't imagine any other country that could possibly have the same reputation in having everyone hear about Princess Diana's death for so much of their lives. I mean, it's almost like Michael Jackson's death, except Michael Jackson's death was not nearly as forced. Princess Diana's death was clearly something people may have gotten sick of. The 59% is still a strong claim. Like, yes, everything's cool with our country, don't even ask that question, there's no need to, because 59 represents a majority. And regardless of how accurate it is, it is so bizarre that it can only possibly be a propaganda element itself. I mean, it's possibly real, but I would have to be convinced that she was really that beloved, and it wasn't just because people like princesses. And you have to really find someone in America that people had the same reaction to, including the press. Michael Jackson doesn't count. It's almost like they wanted to trash him and forget about him. And nobody did forget about him, because he was too popular. That happens a lot in America. And whatever happened in England seems a lot more controlled than that. So convince me on the 59 if you can. And tell me exactly how forced do you think this media presentation was?

**What he means.** The superlative attached to the week is itself suspicious. A number that means “a majority, so stop asking” can do propaganda’s work even if a meter produced it. He wants the 59 percent defended or dropped. He reads the British telling as more controlled than an American star’s death: one mood, repeated, nostalgic, on the side of the Crown, until people are sick of it. *V for Vendetta* is the picture of a government channel hammering that mood. Jackson is the contrast, not the twin. In America the press can try to trash a star and fail, because the public already loved him. In Britain he thinks the love and the coverage were the same machine.

### What “most publicized in human history” actually was

Two different sentences were printed, and they are not the same claim.

On **7 September 1997**, the day after the funeral, Paul McCann in the *Independent* called her “the supreme media creation of the 20th century” and the funeral “the biggest media event in history.” In the same piece he wrote that the worldwide audience was estimated at **2.5 billion** and that the true figure might never be known ([Independent, 7 Sep 1997](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/diana-19611997-the-media-worldwide-coverage-for-the-greatest-media-star-of-all-1237786.html)). The superlative and the caveat came out together. Fürst’s 2020 study found the billions figure running in British papers before the funeral as well as after, usually with no source ([*International Journal of Communication* 14](https://ijoc.org/index.php/ijoc/article/download/11036/3007/44299)). A world record announced before the cameras rolled is a boast.

On **1 October 1997** Durrants said something narrower. In that firm’s British cuttings, back to **1880**, no subject matched the newspaper inches given to her death and the stories after. Kennedy, Lennon, and the single great days of the Second World War fell short ([Independent, 1 Oct 1997](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/media-diana-coverage-set-record-1233239.html)). That is a British newspaper ranking. It is not a count of television, radio, or the planet. It does not say the six years of the war were smaller than one week. It does not survive as “the most publicized event in all of human history.” It does survive as: inside one country’s press archive, this death was the largest single subject they had on file.

### The 59 percent, as a sentence

This file cannot defend 59 percent of the nation. The arithmetic is in [§1.1](#11-how-much-the-press-looked). The audience they printed is about **54 percent** of the **58.3 million** people then living in the UK. One channel had about **19 million**. The trade note stated the millions and then stated “59% of the entire nation” without the division. No worksheet has turned up.

The author’s reading of what that sentence does is fair to the sentence. Fifty-nine is a majority. Printed as “the entire nation,” it answers the question of whether the country was with her, and it answers it shut. A preliminary panel figure that does not divide into the census, published the Monday after as if the country had been measured and found loyal, is a propaganda-shaped line. That does not prove a minister chose the digits. A journalist can divide by a television universe and then write “nation.” Until the worksheet, both are open: a sloppy gloss, or a majority put there because a majority ends the argument. Either way it is not a number this desk will repeat as fact. The number that matches the people is about half.

**Author** (21 Nov 2026, later): he now says he overstated the 59 percent. He treats it as a Nielsen-style rating, a commonly held figure with room for a lot of error, and he reads that looseness as the inflation itself, done for a sense of unity.

The meter he means is real, and it is the millions, not the percent. BARB is the British panel, the same kind of instrument as Nielsen. It published a preliminary average of **31.3 million** and a peak of **31.8 million**, and no later slip has withdrawn them. A panel has sampling error. That error is the ordinary kind. Dividing **31.3 million** by **0.59** gives a denominator of about **53 million**, about five million under the **58.3 million** people in the country. A television universe that leaves out the youngest children can land near **53 million**. So can a slip. The words that do the unity are “59% of the entire nation” and, in the *Independent*, three-quarters of British adults. The share is high by about five points of the whole country, from about **54** to **59**, and the two channels are added into one nation. That is an inflated meaning. It is not an invented audience. “Completely inflated” would be the thirty-one million. The file has the thirty-one million as the preliminary count, and the fifty-nine as the sentence that made the count look like unity.

“She was that beloved” is a separate question from the percent. She was already a television draw before the crash. The same *Independent* that printed 59 percent said **22.8 million** watched the 1995 *Panorama* interview. People liking a princess is not a rebuttal of the week. It is the appetite the week spent. The death did not have to create the habit. McCann called her a media creation the day after she died.

### How forced

The force is in the narrowing of the mood, on a clock, not in a proof that every house was pretending.

Speak to us Ma’am.” The *Sun*: “Where is our Queen? Sean O’Grady, looking back in 2017, called those headlines sickeningly hypocritical, from papers “usually sycophantically loyal,” dodging their own role in the chase, and able to speak that way because they had a public behind them. He also wrote that the coverage and the public reaction were fuelling each other ([Independent, 30 Aug 2017](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/uk/home-news/princess-diana-death-royals-reaction-blair-prince-william-harry-charles-monarch-funeral-mourning-queen-elizabeth-ii-a7918396.html)). The *Independent on Sunday*, that same September, said the press had behaved like children and never had enough of her, and that the paparazzi were chasing her on the papers’ behalf.

Turnock’s interview study, as Fürst uses it, cuts the other way at the same time. Some people were drawn in by seeing the crowds on television and wanted to join. A large share could not identify with the mourning, did not want the coverage, felt the pressure, and kept quiet. Some who did watch thought the scale was too much. The telling was more unanimous than the country.

*V for Vendetta* (2005) is a later picture of a British state channel repeating one patriotic mood until the mood is the public. The likeness is the repetition and the single permitted feeling. The difference is who held the hammer. In that week the papers hammered the palace, the Prime Minister tried to keep the hammer from becoming a blow against the monarchy, and enough people were in the streets that the Queen changed the flag protocol. It was not only a government broadcast landing on a silent public. It was three desks — press, Downing Street, palace — agreeing the emotion, in public, inside six days, and a crowd large enough to be the reason they gave.

Set against an American star’s death, the author’s contrast holds as structure. A country with a handful of national papers, one Crown, and a Prime Minister who wrote down that he was managing the grief for the institution can settle a tone in a week. The American pile-on the author describes, a press trying to trash Jackson and failing because he was already too popular, has more owners and a messier motive. This file has not run the Jackson ratings. The control difference does not need them. Diana’s week had a manager who said what the week was for.

## 5. What the inquest was allowed to find

Operation Paget began **6 January 2004**, after the coroner of the Queen’s Household, Michael Burgess, asked John Stevens to look at allegations of a cover-up: MI6, under royal orders, causing the crash. The basis was Al-Fayed’s public statements ([Wikipedia, Operation Paget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Paget) — convenience summary; the 2006 report is the primary still to pull).

The inquest ran from **2 October 2007** under Lord Justice Scott Baker. On **31 March 2008** he told the jury it was **not open** to them to find unlawful killing by the Duke of Edinburgh, or by anyone else, in a staged accident ([Guardian, 2 Apr 2008](https://www.theguardian.com/uk/2008/apr/02/diana.monarchy1); [Reuters, 1 Apr 2008](https://www.reuters.com/article/world/no-evidence-royals-plotted-to-kill-diana-idUSNOA138953/)). Reuters quoted him: there was no evidence the Duke ordered her execution, and no evidence the security service or any other government agency organised it.

The menu he left them ([CBC](https://www.cbc.ca/news/world/paparazzi-chauffeur-caused-diana-s-death-jury-rules-1.771778); [Guardian, 31 Mar 2008](https://www.theguardian.com/uk/2008/apr/01/diana.monarchy)):

1. Unlawful killing by the grossly negligent driving of the following vehicles.
2. Unlawful killing by the grossly negligent driving of the Mercedes.
3. Both.
4. Accidental death.
5. Open verdict.

On **7 April 2008** the jury, **nine to two**, chose **both**. They added that Paul’s alcohol and the lack of seatbelts contributed. William and Harry said they agreed. The Ministry of Justice said the Crown Prosecution Service could not prosecute foreign nationals for deaths abroad ([BBC](http://news.bbc.co.uk/2/hi/uk_news/7328754.stm)).

So the official British answer is not “accident” in the thin sense. It is **unlawful killing**, aimed at the driver and the photographers, with murder ruled out of the verdict form before the jury sat down to write.

On **18 August 2013** Scotland Yard said it was scoping new information and assessing whether it was credible. It said this was **not** a re-investigation and **not** Operation Paget ([BBC](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/uk-23741483)). What that information was, and what the written outcome was, is an open pull (§10).

### 7.4 *Children of Men* — Baby Diego

That split is the two deaths the author is hearing. Diana’s week was a life lived in cameras, and an inquest that named the photographers as a cause of the crash. The film’s spoken death — refuse the autograph, spit, the fan kills him, the crowd kills the fan — is the Lennon shape. The life-in-pictures, the fame he did not ask for, and the paparazzi frame in the script are the Diana shape. Michael Caine said he based Jasper, the old friend in the woods, on Lennon.
