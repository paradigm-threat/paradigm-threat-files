## 3. Two governments, or one constitution with a veto

**Documented structure.** Article III creates a judicial power. Article II creates the executive. Article I creates Congress. The Constitution on its face is one instrument with three grants, not two free-standing states. *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) is the case in which the Supreme Court held it could refuse to apply an Act of Congress it read as unconstitutional. That holding is the American version of “a judge can say no.”

**Documented counter-tradition inside the founding generation.** Thomas Jefferson, letter to William C. Jarvis, 28 September 1820, called the idea that judges are the ultimate arbiters of all constitutional questions “a very dangerous doctrine indeed, and one which would place us under the despotism of an oligarchy.” Andrew Jackson’s bank veto, 10 July 1832, said the opinion of the judges has no more authority over Congress than the opinion of Congress has over the judges, and that on that point the President is independent of both. Abraham Lincoln’s first inaugural, 4 March 1861, said that if vital policy were irrevocably fixed by Supreme Court decisions in ordinary lawsuits, the people would have ceased to be their own rulers. Those three are the American canon for the author’s “the judge is not the government” sentence. They do not say the courts should be abolished. They say a court opinion is not a statute and is not a command to the other branches acting in their own spheres.

**Author.** The branches are ideological opposites, and neither needs the other to run a whole government. The split exists because the governments of the last three centuries were illegitimate bureaucracies that nobody audited.

**Adjacent documented fact, not the author’s full claim.** Britain ran for a long time without American-style judicial supremacy. Parliament could have the last word. Jeremy Waldron’s argument in [§10](#10-who-else-says-the-court-should-not-have-the-last-word) uses that fact. A parliamentary system is a real government without a Supreme Court that strikes statutes. That supports the narrow claim “a government can operate without this veto.” It does not, by itself, prove the American branches are polar opposites, or that the veto exists only because the governments were evil.

## 5. England, Rome, and the Protestant costume

## 6. Voting for judges

**Documented selection split.** Federal Article III judges are not on the ballot. The President nominates. The Senate confirms. Tenure is during good behavior. State judges are a different government. A 2024 Connecticut Office of Legislative Research summary of the Brennan Center map: for a first full term on a state **trial** court, 19 states use nonpartisan elections and 9 use partisan elections. For a first full term on a state **supreme** court, 26 states use gubernatorial appointment, 14 use nonpartisan elections, and 7 use partisan elections. Sanford Gordon’s 2024 preprint: about 87 percent of the roughly 12,000 state appellate and general-jurisdiction trial judges must face voters at some point. The American Bar Association has preferred appointment from a nominating-commission list since the 1930s, and in 2003 recommended a long single term with no reselection.

**Documented low-information voting.** Philip Dubois, “Voting Cues in Nonpartisan Trial Court Elections,” *Law & Society Review* (1984), found that in low-visibility California trial-court primaries, voters used cues including incumbency, endorsements, and the ethnic and sex signals in surnames. His earlier study of state supreme court elections found that without a party label, judicial voting is weakly structured and can be idiosyncratic. Albert Klumpp’s Cook County work, as reported in the law-review literature, found an Irish-sounding surname worth about 10.5 percentage points in judicial races. Justice Rebecca Wiseman’s study of California retention elections found Hispanic surnames associated with about 1.58 points less of the vote, and “positive” surnames (Best, Armstrong) about 5.7 points better than surnames coded as negative. Ballot roll-off — voters who mark the top of the ticket and skip the judges — is a standard finding in appellate and trial judicial elections. Several states print no party next to the judicial name. Where they do, the party is the cue Dubois found actually structures the vote.

**Documented, Georgia.** Appellate and superior-court vacancies are filled by the governor. The subsequent election is nonpartisan. [Ballotpedia’s 2024 Supreme Court page](https://ballotpedia.org/Georgia_Supreme_Court_elections,_2024): heading into the May 21, 2024 election, every justice except John Ellington (elected 2018) had first been appointed by a Republican governor — Nathan Deal or Brian Kemp. Eight of nine. The contested race was Kemp appointee Andrew Pinson against John Barrow, a former Democratic member of Congress. Ellington, Peterson, and Boggs ran unopposed. Brennan Center, *Appointed and Advantaged* (data as of August 2016): every then-sitting Georgia Supreme Court justice had initially been appointed, not elected. Governor Kemp’s executive order of December 13, 2024, creating the Judicial Nominating Commission, calls the commission “non-partisan, non-political.” The governor appoints its members. Ballotpedia notes he is not bound to its list. A full roster of every 2026 trial-court incumbent is not in this pass.

For Georgia, a Republican governor appoints most of the incumbents now on that list. A challenger is often someone Democrats recruit, or a lawyer whose last public job was in Democratic politics. Voters who know that are voting the party. Voters who do not know it are voting a familiar name, or they are leaving it blank. Almost nobody is voting a neutral “best judge” score, because the ballot does not provide one. The history of the justice system has always been political agenda. Actual justice has been almost entirely neglected.

**Author.** Nobody in the coming election knows which judges to vote for. Red or blue is an instinct the ballot often refuses to print, so the voter needs a pundit. Fatigue produces votes by gender or by last name. The author’s family has historically voted for any name that sounds Jewish, and tells one another to do the same. He does not claim that habit is the national pattern. He claims the ballot makes the habit possible, that any religious or racial bloc can use it, and that immigrants use it often enough that judicial elections became a way to change American law without the prior population’s say. Stacking, on his account, means each side adds justices until the conference votes, and the liberal side usually wins by one or two votes, with planetary consequences. ### H. Beatty Chadwick

**Documented.** In November 1992 Barbara Chadwick filed for divorce in the Delaware County, Pennsylvania, Court of Common Pleas. In February 1993 H. Beatty Chadwick said he had transferred $2,502,000 of the marital estate to a Gibraltar partnership, Maison Blanche, Ltd. On July 22, 1994, the court found the transfers an attempt to defraud his wife and ordered the money paid into escrow. He said he could not comply because the money was lost. In April 1995 he was jailed for civil contempt. Janecka*, 302 F.3d 107 (3d Cir. 2002), reversed a habeas grant and held that indefinite civil confinement was not a federal constitutional bar while state courts found he still had the ability to pay.

**Author.** This is the most prominent modern American example of excessive civil contempt. The “corporate” character of the file is the Gibraltar partnership and the moved assets, inside a divorce, not a separate corporate-dissolution docket.
