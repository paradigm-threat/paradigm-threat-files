## From Ayn Rand to Project RAND

Alisa Rosenbaum left Russia, renamed herself Ayn Rand, and by **1943** had *The Fountainhead* in American culture. The official story of the Air Force think tank that followed is neat: after World War II, Hap Arnold and Douglas Aircraft wanted to keep wartime scientific brainpower, and engineer Arthur Raymond coined **Project RAND** as a contraction of “research and development.” Independent nonprofit in **1948**. Spaceship studies. Systems analysis. Cold War math.

The name still does not sound like a primary name. It sounds like a backronym built so the callsign could be **RAND** while *Fountainhead* was already famous. That is the working read: the generative token is the philosopher’s brand, and “research and development” is the cover expansion. Naming intent will never hand you a signed confession. The functional rhyme is louder. The same postwar restart that tried again to contain Russia also stood up an East-war analytic shop whose callsign rhymes with the Russian émigré who sold Americans a totalizing anti-collectivist machine.

Vietnam was never the master plot. It was a nested theater inside Russia containment — a smaller board inside a larger East fight. RAND ground the war math. Ellsberg worked inside that house.

## McNaughton, Ellsberg, and the Papers

Daniel Ellsberg moved from RAND nuclear work into the Pentagon under Assistant Secretary of Defense John T. McNaughton in **1964**. Tonkin Gulf night put both men inside the traffic as Johnson went to war on a second attack that later SIGINT work would show did not happen as sold. McNaughton shared private assessments Ellsberg was not supposed to broadcast — including the blunt possibility that America was fighting on the wrong side. In **May 1967** McNaughton put moral anguish on paper to McNamara in a way almost unique in the Papers stack. Weeks later McNamara tasked the Vietnam decision-history project that would become the Pentagon Papers. McNaughton was days from swearing as Secretary of the Navy.

In the **2003** film, Ellsberg hits him with the line that still names the job: *You of all people — you’re the hawk that killed the dove.* Period slang: **hawk** means escalate and stay in; **dove** means get out. His memos drafted graduated bombing and provocation scenarios for McNamara — when to hit, what to hit, how to crescendo, how to manufacture grounds to escalate. The document trail makes him look like a driving force for the air war. Privately he told Ellsberg the war was a trap and they should get out on almost any basis. In meetings he still spoke the boss’s hard line. He put Ellsberg on collecting atrocity details so McNamara could push Johnson past one-shot reprisals into systematic bombing. That is what “killed the dove” meant: the pen that buried peace while the man writing it wanted out.

Essentially McNaughton is an antibody wannabe — somebody clearly trying to fight the infection from inside, forced to do the job of the virus instead. He set up the grounds that made Ellsberg the real antibody: the truth-transfer, the study seed, the eyes-only assessments Ellsberg was not supposed to broadcast. That cost McNaughton his life. That is the real, likely outcome for any would-be antibody. As soon as the virus recognizes what they are, it eliminates them before they can become a real threat.

On **19 July 1967**, Piedmont Airlines Flight 22 collided midair near Hendersonville, North Carolina. McNaughton, his wife Sally, his son Ted, and seventy-nine others died. Official packaging called it Cessna pilot error. Period press and NTSB prose from that managed-news culture are not trusted to close the case. The working theory stays locked until definitive contrary proof: McNaughton knew the Vietnam deception truth, told Ellsberg, and was killed for the transfer — wiped before the wannabe could finish becoming the rupture.

FX’s [*The Pentagon Papers*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mEsaa08eFOc) (**2003**) puts that cut on screen and then walks the leak. James Spader plays Ellsberg; Kenneth Welsh plays McNaughton. The film’s emotional hinge is the homecoming: Ellsberg learns McNaughton died in what narration calls a freak plane accident — *He was… gone…* — and the Pentagon sponsor lane closes. Hepatitis, depression, return to RAND, and the McNamara history study waiting in the safes. The rest of the movie is the mechanics of rupture: nights of photocopying classified volumes, the decision to hand them to the press, and the fight that follows when the government tries to stop the presses.

What those volumes actually showed was not one bad night in the Gulf of Tonkin. They were a multi-administration decision history — how Washington planned, expanded, and sold Vietnam while privately tracking that the public story and the war math did not match. Tonkin sat inside a longer pattern of deception. That is why the stack mattered: it was institutional paper proving the country had been marched on a lie, not a single whistleblower’s opinion.

In **1969–1970** Ellsberg copied the study while still cleared at RAND. In **June 1971** the *New York Times* began publishing. Nixon’s Justice Department got a temporary injunction; other papers picked up the leak; Senator Mike Gravel read portions into the Senate record. The Supreme Court case *New York Times Co. v. United States* ended prior restraint. The Papers stayed public. Ellsberg was indicted, then walked free after the White House’s own crimes against him — including the break-in at his psychiatrist’s office — collapsed the prosecution. That **1971** break is the antibody moment McNaughton never lived to see. The **2003** film retells it for a post-9/11 living room while Tonkin’s SIGINT proof was still locked inside NSA.

Ellsberg is not cast here as a saint or a pure hero. He is a historic antibody — an insider pushed too far who turned on his masters. McNaughton was the wannabe who armed him and paid first. RAND clearance culture, McNaughton’s circle, the Papers in the safes, then the rupture. That is historically important without requiring that either man was “good.”
