## From Ayn Rand to Project RAND

Alisa Rosenbaum left Russia, renamed herself Ayn Rand, and by **1943** had *The Fountainhead* in American culture. The official story of the Air Force think tank that followed is neat: after World War II, Hap Arnold and Douglas Aircraft wanted to keep wartime scientific brainpower, and engineer Arthur Raymond coined **Project RAND** as a contraction of “research and development.” Independent nonprofit in **1948**. Spaceship studies. Systems analysis. Cold War math.

The name still does not sound like a primary name. It sounds like a backronym built so the callsign could be **RAND** while *Fountainhead* was already famous. That is the working read: the generative token is the philosopher’s brand, and “research and development” is the cover expansion. Naming intent will never hand you a signed confession. The functional rhyme is louder. The same postwar restart that tried again to contain Russia also stood up an East-war analytic shop whose callsign rhymes with the Russian émigré who sold Americans a totalizing anti-collectivist machine.

Vietnam was never the master plot. It was a nested theater inside Russia containment — a smaller board inside a larger East fight. RAND ground the war math. Ellsberg worked inside that house.

## McNaughton, Ellsberg, and the Papers

Daniel Ellsberg moved from RAND nuclear work into the Pentagon under Assistant Secretary of Defense John T. McNaughton in **1964**. Tonkin Gulf night put both men inside the traffic as Johnson went to war on a second attack that later SIGINT work would show did not happen as sold. McNaughton shared private assessments Ellsberg was not supposed to broadcast — including the blunt possibility that America was fighting on the wrong side. In **May 1967** McNaughton put moral anguish on paper to McNamara in a way almost unique in the Papers stack. Weeks later McNamara tasked the Vietnam decision-history project that would become the Pentagon Papers. McNaughton was days from swearing as Secretary of the Navy.

On **19 July 1967**, Piedmont Airlines Flight 22 collided midair near Hendersonville, North Carolina. McNaughton, his wife Sally, his son Ted, and seventy-nine others died. Official packaging called it Cessna pilot error. Period press and NTSB prose from that managed-news culture are not trusted to close the case. The working theory stays locked until definitive contrary proof: McNaughton knew the Vietnam deception truth, told Ellsberg, and was killed for the transfer.

Ellsberg is not cast here as a saint or a pure hero. He is a historic antibody — an insider pushed too far who turned on his masters.
