That idiot is Shakespeare — a farmed named face turned into a Renaissance **pressure valve**, designed to create a new religion: Nihilism.

Fomenko says Shakespeare wasn’t one single inventing genius, and I agree on the **stories** — they were not born in Stratford from blank cloth. He was the **useful idiot at the valve**: the pick of the litter, farmed or fronted — closed doors barely matter. Any honest chronicler of that material would have told it in its true countries, on its own merit, without the Nihilism agenda. In the end people will remember the line “A tale told by an Idiot,” and stop there.

## The creed

Everyone who sat through high-school English knows the lines, even if they cannot name the act. Life is a walking shadow. A poor player who struts and frets his hour upon the stage. A tale told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, signifying nothing.

That speech sits in *Macbeth*. On stage it belongs to a murderer at the end of his rope. In culture it left the play. Quote banks, book titles, speeches that do not admit they are sermons, and classroom poster walls lift it out as wisdom. Once pulled free of the murderer’s mouth, it stops being “what this character feels.” It becomes a teaching line — like a creed people repeat without noticing they are in church. Reverence is how the teaching is installed. Mockery is the crack.

The core teaching of the new religion is simple: the tale means nothing. Sacred story means nothing. The old empire’s history means nothing. Your life is noise between two silences. Memorize that in poetic English and you have been confirmed.

## Hide the past, own the future

The strategy is simple. It has been obvious for as long as rulers have erased books. If you **hide history** from a people, you **control their future** — because you can force them to **repeat mistakes** they should already know about, lessons they should already have paid for.

War. Famine. Mob rule. The conservation of tradition. Sacred order versus chaos. How empires split. What happens when faith is torn from government, or government from any higher law. A people that still remembers those lessons can refuse the next round. A people taught that the old stories **signify nothing** — or that they were only Italian costume dramas invented by one island genius — walks into the same traps again and calls it progress.

George Santayana said the plain version everyone quotes:

> Those who cannot remember the past are condemned to repeat it.

The Shakespeare project is one machine for making that condemnation stick. Redact the world-empire chronicle. Seal the gap with Nihilism. Flood school and entertainment so the void feels like wisdom. Then the future is not debated — it is herded. The establishment does not need to stand on a balcony and order the next war or the next famine of meaning. It only needs a public that **cannot remember** why the last ones happened.

## The pressure valve — a Renaissance project

School teaches a simple picture. There was a man named William Shakespeare. He lived in Stratford-upon-Avon in England. He wrote the plays out of his own genius. Modern English thought and feeling somehow begin with him. That picture is half true and wholly useful to the redaction.

### What the paperwork actually shows

There **was** a historical William Shakespeare of Stratford. That part is not a rumor. Parish records show a baptism in **1564**. There are marriage, property, tax, and legal records. He appears in London theater and company business. The National Archives holds his **1616 will**, with signatures. Folger’s [Shakespeare Documented](https://shakespearedocumented.folger.edu/about) project gathers hundreds of lifetime and near-lifetime references naming him in manuscripts and print — including title pages and Stationers’ Register entries that treat him as author of poems and plays. By the standards of early modern commoners and working playwrights, that is a **strong paper trail that a man of that name lived, traded, and was publicly tied to the London stage**.

### Could someone else have written it under his name?

Yes — that was not exotic for the age. Early modern London theater was a **production system**. Philip Henslowe’s diary shows that about two-thirds of the plays he recorded were paid to **more than one** playwright. Title pages usually printed **one** name anyway. Companies owned the playbooks, commissioned subjects, and parceled scenes. Publishers later slapped Shakespeare’s name on works specialists now refuse him — including a Stationers’ entry calling *A Yorkshire Tragedy* “written by Wylliam Shakespere,” now widely given to Middleton. Hot names were commodities. There is **no** laboratory proof every Folio line is holograph Shakespeare.

There is a strong contemporary **brand cluster**: title pages, Stationers entries, the Chamberlain’s–King’s Men, the Folio colleagues branding the collected works as his — a solid web for that era, stronger than many peer dramatists. Mainstream treats that as “he wrote them.” Honest reading: the **name and company** locked to the product. It does **not** prove a lone inventing genius, and it does **not** rule out collaboration, house polishing, or farmed sole authorship. He **could** have written every play alone and still been part of the project **without knowing it**. Closed doors are almost of no consequence.

### Farmed, not necessarily ordered

The establishment that needed Scaligerian history protected could not always rule with a visible hand. It needed people **born and raised** to do the wanted work **as if on their own** — early conditioning under British education and print culture: East erased, Easterners filed as Mongol barbarians, island genius installed as summit. Material, patrons, company slot, lucrative path — no sealed order required. He still performs the project.

Same structure as **Tolkien**: both likely wrote the words down; neither invented the upstream world; both had access to material assembled by teams and programs. That access is the proven part. Closed-door psychology is nearly **useless**. There could have been many such people — a **farm**, a litter. Money and prestige went to whoever benefited the redaction most. That pick was Shakespeare. It may have been **the name itself** that qualified him over writers who never made the history books.

### The name that traces to nothing

Eddie Murphy’s Kit Ramsey in *Bowfinger* hears the word the way a child hears it. “It’s not Shakespeare” becomes **shake a spear**. The movie plays it as paranoid comedy. The hearing is still correct. The sacred family name is a transparent English slogan — brandish a spear — not a quiet place-name, not a trade-name your neighbors still live inside, not a clan trail you can walk.

Paper used that string. Medieval and early modern England had nickname surnames of the same type — Shakeshaft, Breakspear, and the rest. That is not the point. The point is what the **brand** leaves in public memory. Most names still point somewhere: a parish, a craft, a family that owned something. **Shakespeare**, as the culture kneels to it, points to a flourish. Shake the spear. Curtain up. If anyone ever bore that name in another country or century — including under colonial renaming — it was a different family, a different reason, and that reason is now forgotten entirely. What remains is a theatrical handle somebody found useful — because it does not drag a grounded legacy into the room. The trail stops at a slogan that sounds like a stage name wearing a baptism record. **Shakespeare traces back to nothing.**

One more paperwork limit: **no surviving handwritten manuscripts** of the plays in his hand (one debated page in *Sir Thomas More* is as close as mainstream gets). The **First Folio** of **1623** is posthumous company branding. Strong attribution. Not proof he invented upstream plots or the meaning of world history.

### The dam

Redacted history is a massive flow. If it is not handled, it bursts — a dam-break of memory that would wreck Scaligerian chronology in public. Shakespeare is the **pressure valve**. Pin the plays to **one person** as the summit of English wisdom, and anyone who challenges the historian arguments inside them must **start by challenging Shakespeare**. The trick worked. Entertainment protected the redacted story; school installed reverence; money followed the brand. The trail stops at Stratford. The empire underneath stays quiet.

We do **not** deny the Stratford man. We deny the leap from “his name is on these English plays” to “one genius invented these stories and the style the world worships.” The **project** is translation and costume — chronicles, earlier plays, sacred stories — rendered so eastern or empire story looks like Rome, Scotland, or Tudor gossip. The **person** is the dam face that makes the package unchallengeable without seeming to attack culture itself.

**Renaissance**, here, means the print-and-culture package that rose with the Reformation and the new European chronology: mass books, national geniuses, school canons, and a managed story of the past — rewrite and valve together.

### Stories from earlier books — and how much “style” is his

Even ordinary professors already know the weaker half of this, and almost never teach it hard in public school. The plots did not start with Shakespeare. *Macbeth* and the English history plays lean on Holinshed’s *Chronicles* — a huge English history book written by a team, not one lonely genius. The Roman plays lean on Plutarch, through Sir Thomas North’s English translation. *Hamlet* sits on the older Danish Amleth story. *Romeo and Juliet* sits on Arthur Brooke’s earlier poem. Specialists then sell “originality” as beautiful language and deep characters, not as inventing the plots. Public school still spends weeks on Shakespeare and usually never teaches that basic fact — that the stories came from earlier books. In my lifetime of classroom Shakespeare, that evidence never arrived. The graduate leaves believing one genius invented the tales.

How much of the famous **writing style** — the quote banks, the walking-shadow speech, the lines people tattoo — can be blamed on those source books instead of on “Shakespeare”? Here the honest answer is: **we do not have a clean public percentage**, and that gap matters.

What we do know:

- **Close verbal borrowing is real.** A classic classroom example is the barge speech about Cleopatra in *Antony and Cleopatra*: it tracks North’s Plutarch so closely that editions call it nearly word for word. Holinshed feeds not only plots but words and phrases into the histories and *Macbeth*. So some of what sounds like “Shakespeare” is already English chronicle or translation prose, lifted and staged.
- **Most stylometry has not answered the credit question.** Computer “style” studies usually compare Shakespeare to other **English playwrights** (Marlowe, Fletcher, and so on). The Claremont Shakespeare Clinic argued the core canon looks like one consistent English voice. The *New Oxford Shakespeare* and related work argue some plays are collaborations (for example Marlowe’s hand on parts of *Henry VI*) — and those claims are contested. Fomenko’s appendix, following Gililov, treats the huge vocabulary as a hint of **several authors**. None of those lanes, as usually run, put Holinshed’s or North’s **source English** in as the control sample and ask: how much of the signature effect disappears when you subtract the borrowed lines?
- **Specialists who police stylometry admit the hole.** Method papers note that source borrowings should be stripped before you measure an author’s style — and that there is **no comprehensive study** listing borrowed words and measuring what happens to “Shakespeare style” when they are removed. Until someone does that carefully, giving Shakespeare sole credit for the linguistic effect of the quote culture is **over-claiming**.

Mainstream teachers still say he “improves” his sources for the stage. Sometimes that is fair for dramatic craft. It is not a license to pretend the sources were mute clay. A large share of the *stories* is upstream. An unknown but real share of the *memorable English* is upstream too. The project then seals the package under one brand and teaches the world to kneel to the brand.

That unfinished measurement is a **next step** of the companion investigation: source-control stylometry against Holinshed/North/Brooke, then residual Folio against eastern/Cyrillic high speech. Armies study Shakespeare; they will not run the fraud-control study. With that many people studying the Bard, it seems the only thing they do not want to study is the **potential of fraud**.

Bacon, Oxford, or English-committee fights keep the **invention** myth alive under a different signature. Swap the valve handle and the dam still stands. Multiple linguistic “hands,” if real, fit a project better than one miracle inventing genius — and still do not, by themselves, prove eastern chronicle.

### Lucrative by design

If this were only about which plays audiences “objectively” love most, rival classical dramatists would share the stage. They do not.

In UK theatre data after the pandemic, Shakespeare still **owns the classical repertoire** — dozens of revivals in a year while no other playwright of that era gets more than a handful. Entire towns live off the brand: Stratford-upon-Avon tourism is measured in hundreds of millions of pounds, with Shakespeare attractions as a primary draw. Canada’s Stratford Festival, built around Shakespeare, generates well over a hundred million dollars in local economic activity. The Royal Shakespeare Company turns over on the order of ninety million pounds and trains generations of actors for whom an RSC credit is career capital. Globally, Shakespeare is treated as the most-performed playwright; festivals and literary tourism export the brand into Asia and beyond.

That is not the same as proving every Shakespeare role pays a higher weekly wage than every contemporary role. Musicals often take the biggest box-office share. Straight Folio nights often ride **subsidy, school compulsion, tourism, and prestige**. Those are exactly the levers you pull if the goal is to keep artists pushing **this** media style for centuries — protecting redacted history — rather than letting rival chronicles compete on a level field. When the money and status flow to the dam, the dam does not need to win a pure popularity contest against every musical. It only needs to own the slot where serious culture and serious careers go.

So yes: on this evidence, Shakespeare was **unfairly sponsored** by rich and institutional sources across the legacy. It is easy to argue that these plays, these versions, and the man himself would have been **outcompeted and largely forgotten** without that same influence — the fate that did hit his peers. What **cannot honestly** be argued is that the dominance pattern was **earned by the skill and quality of one person’s writings**. Skill may exist; sole-cause merit does not explain centuries of monopoly. At the very least, the skill camp must admit the public was not sold **remakes** of Holinshed, North, Brooke, and the rest. They were sold **original plays** by a genius. That presentation is the fraud floor even before Empire maps enter the room.

## World empire stories — what New Chronology reconstructs

School sells Shakespeare as isolated theater: a sad prince in Denmark, a bloody thane in Scotland, lovers in Verona, senators in Rome — a little Italy and Britain of the mind. Private tales. Local color. One island genius inventing psychology for modernity. Each play sits in its own little country box. Denmark has nothing to do with Russia. Scotland has nothing to do with the empire. Italy is just Italy. That is how the classroom keeps the world from connecting.

The Russian-born Bulgarian economist, sociologist, and theoretician **Ivan Germanovich Kinkel** (1883–1945) named the deeper error behind that habit:

> The deepest mistake of modern historiography lies in the study of individual countries in complete isolation from one another. Society cannot be understood in fragments; economic and cultural evolution is an interconnected, unified process where no nation develops behind a closed wall.

That is the same mistake the Shakespeare project made. And it made that mistake under the instructions of Renaissance dogma — which specifically made this mistake **everywhere**, and **retroactively**, through its Scaligerian redaction of true human history. Scaligerian redaction means the dating-and-mapping system that stretches, duplicates, and relocates one connected world story into separate “ancient” nations and separate “local” dramas. Once countries are studied behind closed walls, empire chronicle can be sold as Italian novella, Danish ghost story, and Scottish tragedy — and nobody is supposed to notice it is one process.

**New Chronology** is the reconstruction work of Anatoly Fomenko and Gleb Nosovskiy (and colleagues). In plain terms: they argue that the standard “ancient” timeline is stretched and duplicated, and that many famous stories are recent world history wearing wrong dates and wrong maps. In [*What Shakespeare Actually Wrote About*](https://chronologia.org/en/shakespeare/index.html), they read the famous plays as based on real events of the twelfth through sixteenth centuries inside the **Great Empire** — a Eurasian world order often called Rus–Horde in this research — then wrongly dated and moved west by the Scaligerian geography textbooks still use. *Hamlet*, *Macbeth*, *Lear*, *Henry VIII*, *Timon*, *Titus* and the rest are not random Italian novellas. They are **world-empire stories**. The entire world heard them because the entire world was affected by them. No nation developed that history behind a closed wall — which is why isolating the plays by country is not neutral teaching. It is the redaction’s method.

That is the reconstruction’s context in plain speech. These were not boutique entertainments for one city’s rich audience. They were the shared memory of a planetary civilization working through new contradictions and conflicts — and coming to agreements on their merit. A true path of evolution of the species: blood, succession, betrayal, sacred kingship, gospel-shaped chronicle, the cost of splitting a realm. People argued about what the stories meant because the stories named what had just happened to everyone.

The Renaissance Shakespeare project kept the costumes and dropped the conclusions. Messages that mattered — especially where people still have faith in their religion, or believe that Christianity is linked to government — did not survive the English rewrite as usable teaching. What survived was psychology, pretty speeches, and the void creed. Empire chronicle became “a tale told by an idiot.” Faith tied to rule became stage intrigue. The agreements the species had already paid for in history were filed as fiction from a faraway country.

Fomenko’s reconstruction revives what the rewrite buried: the plays as **encoded chronicle** — history hidden inside drama. Put them back on the world-empire map and hear the conclusions again — including the ones Nihilism was designed to erase. The chart’s strongest rows (*Hamlet*, *Macbeth*, *Lear*, *Henry VIII*) already make “Italian story” unserious. The globe was the stage because the globe had lived the plot.

## Nihilism as the new religion

**Nihilism** here is not a teenage mood. It is a religion.

It is the rejection of all religion. Of all structured order to the universe. Of all hope for humanity. Of faith in anything.

People say that is not a religion — that it is anti-religion, or atheism, or “just realism.” The dodge fails. You cannot fill the gap of human history with simple notions of dismissing. Multitudes of intersecting themes stay essential in anyone’s value system or belief system. People who say they believe in nothing still have a belief system. Even if the content is nothing. Believing in nothing is still belief.

Between Christianity and nothing, English print culture chose nothing and made the choice sound like art. The Renaissance project did not hand out pamphlets titled *How to Stop Believing*. It required literature. It required stage and later film. It required exams. It omitted the traditions and religions of the existing and old world, then filled the silence with void speeches, suicidal deliberation, mockery of conscience, and wonder at a “brave new world” that readers later recognized as managed order.

That is obligatory high culture — not a sect with a building, a creed with a curriculum.

*Here is a little fake “Shakespeare style,” then plain speech again:*

> What is a man, if all his yesterdays light fools the way to dusty death? What is an empire, if the chronicle be but sound and fury? Signify nothing, and thou shalt be counted wise; keep faith with the old world, and thou art provincial.

That took half a minute. The point is not that old-fashioned verse is magic. The point is that the “high style” is a costume you can put on. The religion underneath is the void.

## What was omitted

The old world had traditions, religions, imperial Christianity, eastern memory, and a cosmos that was not random noise. The Reformation’s cultural war first made that order look foolish. Sebastian Brant’s *Ship of Fools* (and the carnival “hell wagon” shows that went with that mood) trained Europe to laugh at the empire as a boat of idiots and to storm it. When the empire was split, the same shows were later filed as harmless medieval humor.

Then the cleared century needed replacement “great works.” Famous names — painters, mapmakers, and the English playwright — got later works attributed to them in the spirit of the new school chronology. Shakespeare is the English seal of that replace phase: ridicule first, then obligatory genius.

What dropped out of the Shakespeare rewrite was not only place-names. It was **value**. Conclusions the empire stories carried — about faith, about sacred order, about whether Christianity and government belong in one body of memory — were the parts least likely to be carried over intact. A play that still hides Herod and Christ underneath can still teach a classroom that the tale signifies nothing. That is the point of the seal. Keep the sound and fury. Lose the merit of the agreements.

Silence about the empire and worship of the Bard travel together. One classroom withholds the world; the other installs the void. New Chronology is one path back to the lost conclusions; Nihilism filled the gap while they were gone.

## Museum irony

Here is the first hard contrast.

The West built a **museum** around Shakespearean metaphor. School makes you kneel to it. Entertainment refreshes it. Talking ill of Shakespeare will not win you many friends. The same West spent the last three to five centuries of the Renaissance Revolution teaching living speakers that metaphor, deep abstraction, and surreal density are inappropriate — a bit much — uncool — unserious.

Eastern languages spoke that way as ordinary high speech. Metaphor was not a museum stunt. Thinking in deep images was not a social mistake. Literature written in Cyrillic and other eastern tongues carried that poetic way of talking as how people thought on the page. After Latin-based Western prestige won, and eastern tongues were filed as “ancient” or “dead,” English speakers met that density mainly inside the Shakespeare collected works — and credited a Stratford person. They should have been impressed by Cyrillic literature in general. The [Hidden History of Words](https://paradigmthreat2.substack.com/p/the-hidden-history-of-words) already pins Shakespeare, Bacon, and the King James Bible print elite as the seal on English after the Horde empire was broken. Eastern origin under that seal is the sister claim.

Modern English, after that war, is rigid. It names the world like a government form. It distrusts image. It rewards thin clarity and punishes speech that still sounds like it is touching something. The museum keeps one frozen sample of metaphor as compulsory genius. The street is told not to talk that way.

*Another fake “Shakespeare” costume, then the contrast in ordinary words:*

> Soft! What light through eastern casements breaks? It is the tongue that named the heavens as kin, and man as more than dust rehearsing nothing. Yet in the West we cage that light in folios, and call the living speaker fool.

The museum irony is the beat that sticks. The Revolution that installs Shakespeare empties the street of the eastern way of speaking. Sacred costume upstairs. Living metaphor downstairs marked unserious. That is not taste evolving. That is a culture war with a trophy case.

## France: war, not taste

Here is the second hard contrast.

Shakespeare’s impact on France did not begin as worship. It began as trouble. Voltaire helped introduce him in the 1730s, then turned against him. He called the English dramatist strong and fruitful — and without taste, without the French rules of drama, a ruin of the English stage, a “barbarous actor.” He translated passages, then spent decades warning Europe that English excess would wreck French theater. Eighteenth-century France rewrote and cut “ugly” scenes to fit classical rules (one place, one day, one plot; no mixing kings with clowns). Gravediggers and cobblers on stage with kings offended a theater that wanted elevation and moral clarity. Ducis rewrote *Hamlet* until it behaved for a French audience. For a long stretch, rejection was the official polite posture — not a fringe blog, the house style of letters.

Then the Romantic century reversed the field. New French translations and a craze for English things opened the door. Shakespeare became the liberating model against the French Academy’s strict taste. The fight over Victor Hugo’s play *Hernani* and Hugo’s campaign turned excess and the grotesque into a banner. Critics who had treated Shakespeare as the enemy of taste found themselves on the losing side of a fashion that became an orthodoxy. By the end of the arc, the same figure who had been barbaric was canonized as universal genius.

That sequence is not ordinary taste discovering a timeless classic. Taste that needs a century of institutional hostility, then a Romantic street fight, then total school-and-stage acceptance is not “we finally noticed beauty.” Rejection first, then obsession, then near-total acceptance means somebody won. Winner-take-all cultural war, sponsored by major influences of the time, fills the field until rival canons starve. France is the continental specimen of the same war English schools and stages ran at home.

French-style Nihilism — literary despair, void philosophy, the continental mood that later looked native to Paris — sits downstream of that English project once France took in and made holy the Bard. The creed crossed the Channel as literature, then returned as “French” despair with English roots. When later readers blame Paris for inventing nothingness, they are often reading an export that France first refused and then over-installed.

France proves war, not taste. Taste does not require a century of institutional hostility followed by forced conversion. Victory does.

## How it reached nearly everyone

The Renaissance / Shakespeare package flooded **both** education and entertainment. Classroom time made it required. Stage, film, tourism, and quote culture made it feel like leisure. Places where English literature is the prestige language treat knowing Shakespeare as non-optional civilization. Not every person reads the First Folio cover to cover. Nearly all are touched by the brand.

That is control by filling the field first. Install one summit of wisdom so rival histories never get a fair hearing. The establishment does not always need to ban competing opinions. It floods school and entertainment until competing opinions have no status and no friends.

Talking ill of Shakespeare still costs status in polite English-speaking society — soft policing after the install. France showed the war while the field was contested; the English dual flood shows the war after victory, when dissent reads as anti-culture rather than anti-propaganda.

## Science as a second liturgy

Here is the third hard contrast.

Before the modern popular picture hardened, many cultures treated the heavens as ordered, meaningful, inhabited, or at least structured. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century science — as public culture, not as every working equation — popularized void as realism. Vacuum of space as the default room the stars sit in. Black holes as the glamorous nothing that eats light. A chaotic Newtonian picture in which the human race is alive purely by chance and could be wiped out by an asteroid at any time. From there it is a short step to the lonely-universe mood: no other life, nowhere, ever — the planet as accident. Lars von Trier’s [*Melancholia*](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt1527186/) is a late pop sacrament of that feeling: beauty, depression, and a world ending because the cosmos does not care.

This is **science as a second church service**. Same religion, lab coat instead of poetic speech. The Macbeth creed — signifying nothing — starts to feel like what educated people “know” about physics and astronomy. An ordered cosmos looks provincial. Chance void looks mature. Hope becomes a counseling problem. Faith becomes a private hobby. The asteroid is the new Last Judgment with no judge.

Newton did not write for Stratford. The claim is not a forged memo linking his *Principia* to Shakespeare’s collected plays. The claim is the same religion in two outfits, with the same outcome. Once English literature trained the English-speaking world to rehearse nothingness as wisdom, French literary Nihilism and scientific empty-cosmos talk had a prepared congregation. Realism helped replace an ordered universe with a universe where hope looks childish. School teaches both services: the quote from *Macbeth*, then the poster of empty space.

Museum irony froze metaphor in a trophy case. France proved the culture war. Science baptized the void as fact. Three doors, one creed: **Nihilism**.

## The human cost

Through literature the project filled people with Nihilism while omitting the traditions and religions of the existing and old world. The reachable human race became largely disillusioned, depressed, warlike, evil, and lost.

The canon itself rehearses the symptoms. Hamlet’s world is weary, stale, flat, and unprofitable. Flesh would melt. A bare bodkin ends the coil. Macbeth grows aweary of the sun and wishes the estate of the world undone. Edmund worships Nature against custom. Iago invents divinity of hell. Richard is himself alone. School treats these as character studies. Culture treats the most quotable void lines as insight.

A population that rehearses meaninglessness becomes easier to rule. Ignorance feels adult; self-rule looks naive. The “brave new world” — lifted from *The Tempest*, later a dystopian title — is what remains when the old world is filed as idiot noise or fantasy. Mud-flood / empire fracture destroy memory in the ground; the Renaissance project teaches survivors the tale never meant anything anyway.

That is the cost. Not a bad play. A civilization trained to accept nothing as its faith — and therefore condemned to repeat what it was forbidden to remember.
