# Malcolm McDowell: the villain role that did not let go

> **TL;DR:** After *A Clockwork Orange* (1971) and *Caligula* (1979), Malcolm McDowell’s name stays attached to a villain the public already hates. He keeps working. The jobs get smaller: a week’s pay on a film he never sees, direct-to-video science fiction, and, in 1994, Dr. Tolian Soran in *Star Trek Generations*, the man who kills Captain Kirk. Fans send death threats. He says both versions of the death were feeble, and that the producers should have given Kirk a glorious end. Paramount had already spent about **$3 million** reshooting the climax after test audiences rejected Soran shooting Kirk in the back. **Author:** this is celebrity containment — the same man at the center of a second controversy, unable to break the role. ## 2. Clockwork Orange, the role

**Documented.** *A Clockwork Orange* (1971), Stanley Kubrick. McDowell is Alex DeLarge. In February 1996 he told *Radio Times* that for years he resented the impact the film had on his life, and that he became a world star typecast as a baddie. Lindsay Anderson, after *If....* (1968), had told him it was downhill from there. McDowell’s own account of the years after is that the British film industry collapsed, that he does not try to be liked, and that “loads of it is crap.” [Radio Times, February 1996](http://www.geocities.ws/malcolminterviews/radio296.html).

**Author:** Alex is the role he does not break. The later villains are the same job with a new costume. Resenting the impact, and still taking the villain week, is the containment sentence.

## 3. Caligula

**Documented.** He plays the title. After the actors wrapped, Bob Guccione spliced in hardcore scenes. McDowell’s line on the finished film, as *The Independent* prints it: “I advise people never to see it. It is a terrible film: exploitive and pornographic.” He says the cut depressed him. In November 2007 he tells *Girls of Penthouse* that he is proud of his own work and that Guccione’s inserts were “an absolutely outrageous betrayal.” Helen Mirren, as Caesonia, does not denounce the film. That split, and what each career could still sell, is in [Helen Mirren §9](/influence/conspiracy/celebrities/helen-mirren.md#9-controversies) and the reader essay.

**Author:** *Caligula* is the first time he is put at the center of a controversy he then has to wear. Telling the public not to see it did not free the name. The name stayed the controversy.

**Author:** that is the containment. Not a missing lead for one season. A decade in which the former Alex is the villain-for-hire, and the only large film puts him back at the center of a public fight.

## 5. Star Trek Generations

*Star Trek Generations* opened **18 November 1994**. McDowell is Dr. Tolian Soran. The picture is the handoff from William Shatner’s Kirk to Patrick Stewart’s Picard. Kirk dies.

### Shot in the back, then the bridge

**Documented.** As first written and first shot, Soran kills Kirk by shooting him in the back. Ronald D. Moore, on the *Generations* DVD commentary, says it was always expected that Kirk would die on the bridge of his ship, so he and Brannon Braga went the other way. Moore’s example is John Wayne in *Sands of Iwo Jima* (1949), shot by a sniper just after the men declare victory, and he says he enjoyed the irony of it. In the later documentary account, Moore says Kirk “dies in an unexpected way, and there’s a tragedy and irony to that,” and Braga says, “We were gonna do this kind of moment where Kirk’s shot in the back.” Director David Carson fought the staging, lost, and called it “an antiquated gunfight.” Shatner, Stewart, and Carson asked the studio from the set to rewrite it. The studio did not, until a test screening. Sherry Lansing then ordered a new ending without moving the release date. Paramount sent the company back for about two weeks. The *Los Angeles Times*, **13 November 1994**, reports that the studio spent **$3 million** to reshoot a seven-minute ending, because the finale was the weak point and Paramount wanted more heroism from Kirk. The released death is the collapsing bridge. *Dreamwatch*, in February 1995, reports that the reshoot of the last fifteen minutes was so late that the published novelization could not be changed and still has the original, less noble death, and that television promos carried dialogue cut from the film. The *Times* described the reshoot five days before the opening. The theaters did not show the shot in the back. The book still printed it. [TrekBBS, on the Moore/Braga commentary](https://www.trekbbs.com/threads/was-the-obsession-with-passing-the-torch-what-scuttled-generations.137280/page-3); [Inverse, on the documentary](https://www.inverse.com/entertainment/star-trek-generations-kirk-death-ad-lib); [Carson](https://trekmovie.com/2011/05/12/star-trek-generations-director-carson-on-reshooting-death-of-kirk/); [LA Times](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1994-11-13-ca-62247-story.html); [Dreamwatch, February 1995](http://protovision.textfiles.com/sf/STARTREK/generat.rev).

### He agreed it was weak

**Documented.** McDowell does not defend either ending. “We shot it twice. It was feeble, I thought, because I thought [Kirk] deserved better.” “The producers should have given him a much better sendoff.” “They should have given him a glorious death. If they’re going to kill one of the icons of American television, then get rid of him in a beautiful way. I was very disappointed.” On the reshoot itself: “Me shooting the bridge out or some BS whatever it was… they spent several million dollars… I thought it was poor, very poor, even the reshoot.” And: “Whoever came up with his death at the end, I thought it was really cheesy… he should have been given a blazing death. And I, of course, would have been happy to have supplied that.” Shatner’s joke to him, which McDowell repeats, is “You shot me in the back.” [TrekToday](https://trektoday.com/content/2012/09/mcdowell-kirk-deserved-better/); [StarTrek.com](https://www.startrek.com/news/malcolm-mcdowell-killed-kirkundefined-but-hated-it-part-ii); [TV Series Finale](https://tvseriesfinale.com/tv-show/star-trek-generations-kirk-death-20125/).

The “bridge drop” line in the author’s note is this complaint. He is not proud of the death the studio paid to improve.

### Death threats

**Documented.** He says the threats were real, and that this was the early internet, so a threat was easy and hollow. In a 2010 *Mental Floss* interview he says he did not take them seriously and the studio did: two detectives accompanied him to New York for the press, including a night at the Carlyle, and he found the street empty. “I feel embarrassed that nobody’s tried to kill me.” The *Radio Times* in February 1996 already records death threats from “Trekkies” for killing Kirk. [Slashfilm, citing Mental Floss 2010](https://www.slashfilm.com/1459513/star-trek-generations-captain-kirk-death-malcolm-mcdowell-fan-threats/); [StarTrek.com](https://www.startrek.com/news/malcolm-mcdowell-killed-kirkundefined-but-hated-it-part-ii); [Radio Times, February 1996](http://www.geocities.ws/malcolminterviews/radio296.html).

**Author:** the fans punish the actor for a death the producers staged, reshot, and released. He agrees with the fans that the death was unworthy, and he still wears it. That is the containment: the controversy is his public body, and the choice of how the icon died was not his.

## 6. James Woods, the other sentence

> i can see woods being versatile enough to choose the path mcdowell didn't - to reserve criticism to avoid being reduced to b villian roles, and take shoddy rolls like final fantasy spirits within, and survive to do things he actually wanted to do

**Documented.** James Woods, after *The Onion Field* (1979), was the villain the studios kept hiring. In 1986 he told Don Shewey he had spent a year or two trying to avoid villains, and that *Best Seller* was “my farewell to villainy,” taken because the part was too good to refuse. In January 1997 he told the *Los Angeles Times* that if an actor has to be typed, “better for the bad guy than for anything else,” because the parts last and are more intriguing. [Shewey, 1986](https://www.thestacksreader.com/out-of-the-woods/); [LA Times, 1 January 1997](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1997-01-01-ca-14352-story.html).

*Final Fantasy: The Spirits Within* opened in the United States on **11 July 2001**. Woods is the voice of General Douglas Hein, the officer who wants the Zeus cannon used on the Phantoms. The picture cost **$137 million** and grossed **$85.1 million**. It is filed as a box-office bomb and is blamed for the closing of Square Pictures. Alec Baldwin, Donald Sutherland, Steve Buscemi, and Ving Rhames are on the same call sheet. No interview gathered here has Woods telling the audience not to see it. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Final_Fantasy:_The_Spirits_Within).

In 2006 he took *Shark*, his first series. He told *Variety* he had turned series down, and that he was not that interested in working, until this script. “To read this unusual story that portrayed a middle-aged white male as a hero rather than an idiot.” “It’s fun playing a hero.” “This is the first time in 10 years I read anything that had a really exciting, edgy character who fits my description.” [Variety, 2007](https://variety.com/2007/scene/awards/emmy-contender-james-woods-1117966483/).

**Author:** that is the path McDowell did not take. McDowell’s public line on *Caligula* is “never see it,” and his public line on Kirk’s death is that it was feeble. Woods kept that kind of sentence off the pictures he was in, took a failed villain job at studio scale, and was still in a position to accept the lead he describes as the one he had been waiting on. The motive — silence as the price of not being dropped into the B-villain week — is the author’s reading. The page shows the missing denunciation, the bomb, and the series.

## 7. Author’s open claims

1. The post-1971 career is containment in the villain role, not a free tour of genres.
2. *Caligula* and *Generations* are the two times a major film puts him at the center of a controversy he then cannot put down.
3. The B-movie and direct-to-video slate is the evidence that he did not break out. *Exquisite Tenderness* — paid, unseen — is the clearest single specimen.
4. No interview gathered here is a signed confession that a contract forced a named title. The force, on this reading, is the role the market would still buy.
5. James Woods kept a “never see it” sentence off his own pictures, took General Hein in *Final Fantasy: The Spirits Within*, and was still offered *Shark*, the lead he says he wanted.
