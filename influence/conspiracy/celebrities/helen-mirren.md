## 3. The house: Gzhatsk, the arms mission, the name

**Smolensk, in her own account.** In 2004 she told *The Telegraph* that her grandfather “was in the Tsarist army and had been sent to London to do an arms deal with the British government during the Russo-Japanese war,” and that he “left behind six sisters who lived on the family estate at Gzhatsk, near Smolensk.” The sisters, on the letters she had just found, left the estate during the revolution for rooms in Moscow. [Telegraph, 2004](https://www.telegraph.co.uk/culture/3615659/Mirrens-search-for-her-Moscow-roots.html). Gzhatsk is the town later renamed **Gagarin**, in Smolensk Oblast.

**The papers in London.** University College London holds the Mironov collection. Colonel **Pyotr Vasilievich Mironov** was a military engineer seconded to Britain to buy arms for the Russian army, particularly during the First World War. He served on the **Russian Government Committee in London**, the body that commissioned and shipped armaments. After the revolution he stayed. He and Colonel Belaiew and other officers worked on the **Russian Liquidation Committee**, which the British Treasury used to assess what Russia still owed. [UCL SSEES](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/library/ssees-archives/mrn.htm).

**Two clocks for the arrival.** Mirren’s 1994 and 2004 interviews put the arms deal in the **Russo-Japanese War** (1904–05). The UCL description puts the committee work in the **1914–18** war. Her father was a small child on the trip that did not reverse. A 1904 mission and a wartime committee job can both be true only if the colonel came more than once. The stranding itself is not in dispute. In 1994 she said: “He would have been executed if he’d gone back. All his life he said Communism wouldn’t last.” [Independent, 1994](https://www.helen-mirren.net/articles/199403theindependent/). *People*, drawing on her memoir and a 2006 *New Yorker* profile, gives the estate as **Kuryanovo**, about fifteen hundred acres, lost with the homeland and the pedigree. [People, 24 Aug 2025](https://people.com/helen-mirren-s-dad-changed-their-russian-last-name-to-assimilate-exclusive-11790587). Gzhatsk and Kuryanovo are both in print. This file does not yet have the chest inventory that would say whether those are the town and the house, or two stories.

**“Diplomat.”** The author’s word is Tsarist diplomat. The archive’s word is colonel, military engineer, government purchasing committee. That is a state mission with embassy quarters, in the family’s telling. It is not a foreign-ministry rank on the UCL card. The political fact the author needs is still there: a Tsarist officer on the Crown’s doorstep, then a revolution, then no road home.

**The name, on a child already born in the West.** She did not emigrate. The previous generation did, by being unable to leave. *People* (24 Aug 2025) quotes her: “One minute I was called Mironov, and then, Mirren.” The reason, she said, was that her father “wanted to assimilate.” Britain then was “a pretty monoculture sort of place with very few foreign names.” The memoir line, as *People* prints it, is that he “dropped the foreign Mironov for the more acceptable though falsely Scottish Mirren.” The 1994 profile says that up to the age of ten she was still **Miss Helen Mironoff**. [Independent, 1994](https://www.helen-mirren.net/articles/199403theindependent/). So the child learns one surname, and the English one arrives while she is still at school.

Her mother was **Kathleen Rogers**, an East End butcher’s daughter. The press house is working-class Cockney on one side and a stranded Russian officer’s line on the other. [LA Times](https://projects.latimes.com/hollywood/star-walk/helen-mirren/). The father drove a taxi, played viola, and became a driving examiner. She has said the civil-service job required him to say he was born in England, and that he lied about that. [Independent, 1994](https://www.helen-mirren.net/articles/199403theindependent/).

**Author:** that is the stage. A Smolensk estate, a Tsarist arms errand, a liquidation committee, a false Scottish name, a birth in Chiswick. The child is already inside the West, with a usable Russian past that can be hidden or performed. The later parts — the Queen, the Empress, Golda — are what that availability is spent on.

## 5. Burnout, on the record, and the work that followed

The author’s main argument is burnout. She does not seem to like the work. She continues. The sentences are hers, and they repeat across thirty years.

**1996**, about age fifty, to Ruby Wax, as *The Independent* reported it: “I don’t think I want my career to go on and on. I want to retire quite soon.” She wanted the English countryside and “bad pictures of horrible little landscapes.” She was “sick of the ambition” and of the feeling that she was doing it terribly. Then: “I’m really quite happy not to work.” [Independent, 1996](https://www.independent.co.uk/news/mirren-to-quit-acting-for-quiet-life-1282393.html).

**2006**, at the crest of *The Queen*, from Los Angeles, as the *Evening Standard* has it: “I’ve lost my idealism. I’ve lost my sense of vocation — that you were fulfilling a serious and necessary function in your culture. It just slowly slipped away.” And: “I’m in a weird place just now. Of not being sure I ever want to work again. I want to re-find the desire to pretend to be somebody.” [Evening Standard](https://www.standard.co.uk/showbiz/i-may-never-act-again-says-helen-mirren-7191149.html). *Prime Suspect* still had another series. The damehood was already three years old. The Oscar campaign was underway.

**2015**, *Radio Times* via *The Guardian*: stopping “would be quite nice.” “I don’t think ‘dying on the job’ is such a wonderful thing.” In the same conversation she gives the other motor: “I’m driven by competitiveness, always thinking I can do better, and the sheer pleasure of earning a living.” [Guardian, 31 Mar 2015](https://www.theguardian.com/media/2015/mar/31/helen-mirren-women-film-tv).

**The Fast years.** She told *Entertainment Weekly* that most of her career had been art-house, “and they’re the movies that I personally enjoy,” and that *Fast & Furious* was another world she had not known. She had expected to drive. For two of the three films she did not. [EW](https://ew.com/movies/helen-mirren-fast-furious-glee-queen-role-call/).

**2025.** On a slate that includes *1923*, *MobLand*, and *The Thursday Murder Club*, she told *The Hollywood Reporter*: “Don’t get me wrong, I love not working, and especially now more than earlier, I feel I’ve earned it; I deserve it.” Then: if there is a project she wants, and she can still do it, she will. [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/movies/movie-news/helen-mirren-many-projects-thoughts-on-retiring-interview-1236345423/).

**Author:** read the stack in order. At fifty she is happy not to work. At sixty-one, in the middle of the Queen film, the vocation is gone and she is not sure she wants to pretend to be somebody. At seventy she would like to stop, and she takes the car franchise anyway. At eighty she loves not working and is in several shows at once, including the woman who founded the Israeli state. A person who can refuse does not need thirty years of public farewells. The competitiveness line and the “if I want it, I’ll do it” line are also hers. They describe a worker who still says yes. They do not erase the earlier sentences, and they do not name a pension, a contract, or a silence clause. No public paper says she is forbidden to retire. The author’s claim is the pattern: the wish to stop is on the record, and the political parts keep arriving.

The dubious productions, on this reading, are not *Caligula* alone. They are the state faces. *Elizabeth I*, *The Queen*, *The Audience*, *Catherine the Great*, *The Debt*, *Woman in Gold*, *Golda*. The car films are the late odd jobs. The queens are the job.

### The costume tears, and the sentence that follows them

The crying story is not a fan invention. It is an awards-season set piece, and it is always finished with a yes.

John Lahr, *The New Yorker*, 2 October 2006, “Command Performance”:

> “I cried when I did my costume fitting,” Mirren said. “I saw the shoes lined up and the tweed skirts and the sweaters. I said, ‘I can’t do this.’ But I grew to love it.”

The next sentences, in the same profile, supply the approved meaning. The Queen’s “complete lack of vanity was a comfortable place to be as a woman.” Then: “I loved being her.” Then: “I thought I had a lot in common with her.” Then she says she admires the Queen “as an actress,” and that “there are elements of that little girl in me. The sense of wanting to do the right thing, not wanting conflict.” [New Yorker, 2 Oct 2006](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2006/10/02/command-performance-3). *Variety*’s awards piece the same season has her looking at the tweed, the sweaters, the sensible shoes, and the iron-gray wig, and crying, and saying “I can’t do this,” before she has stepped in front of the camera. [Variety, 2006](https://variety.com/2006/film/awards/regal-thesp-in-her-prime-1117954450/).

**Author:** the tears are the dubious part of the package, because the package tells you what the tears were for. Shoes. Tweed. A wig. A woman who has already played Elizabeth I, the year before, and who will play this queen’s weekly audiences again in *The Audience* (2013), is presented as overcome by a Barbour-jacket wardrobe, and then as a woman who loved being her and had a lot in common with her. “I can’t do this” is a refusal. “But I grew to love it” is the correction, delivered in the same breath, on the Oscar tour. The Jubilee interview has her hoping Charles will be king. Read as a person changing her mind, the stack is possible. Read as a person who did not want the role, the stack is a refusal and then a run of public statements that she did. This file takes the second reading as the one to test. No one has produced an order that compelled the fitting. What is on the page is the shape of being pushed through it: she says she cannot, she does it, and the press is given the love.

The same profile has an older quip that does the wanting in advance: “I don’t mind if I don’t have any lines as long as I get to wear a crown.” Frears says it may have been the part she was waiting for “in some unspoken way.” That is the public desire. ## 13. Weak points

- **Diplomat** is the author’s noun. The archive says colonel and military engineer on a purchasing committee.
- **Kiev and Smolensk** are a rhyme of region, not one hometown. Golda’s childhood papers are a different file.
- **She did not move west.** Her grandfather’s stranding is why she is born there. The name change is her father’s, in England, when she is a child.
- **“No choice”** is the pattern of farewell-plus-the-next-part. She also says she takes work she wants, and that pay and competition keep her in. No one has produced a clause that jails her if she stops.
- **6 October / 7 October 2023** is a UK release date beside a war. The film was already in American theaters in August. Do not make the calendar into a trigger.
- **AU-048 does not apply.** Nothing in this file is a natal-sex claim. Do not move her onto that gate because she played Golda.
- **“Soviet-era Russian”** is Dwight’s line. The family story in print is Tsarist and anti-Communist, and the birthplace is Chiswick. The author’s use of the line is the compromise label, not a finding that she was born in the USSR.
- **“Not a citizen of any country”** is political, not a passport fact. She was born in Britain and was made a Dame. The claim is that the protected international life is the real residence.
- **The Gaza sentence (2026) is also hers.** Filing the Zionism as true does not delete “crimes against humanity.” It refuses the idea that the eternity line and the Golda film were an internet rumor.
- **“I grew to love it” is also hers.** Reading that sentence as a required correction is the author’s test of the costume story. It is not a second recording of someone telling her what to say after the fitting. The tears, the shoes, and the love line are one *New Yorker* paragraph.
- ***Excalibur* was not a casting scandal.** No 1981 campaign said a Russian woman must not play Morgana. The English citizenship is why the part looked ordinary. The Arthur reading is this desk’s, cross-linked above, not a line Boorman gave the press.
- **Elizabeth I’s bloodline is the open dupe.** The vassal court, the Tudor–Theodore rhyme, and the breakaway fragment are already in AU-049, on Fomenko. “She was part of that family” is the author’s next step. It is not a birth record, and it is not a claim that Mirren and Elizabeth I share a Smolensk deed.
