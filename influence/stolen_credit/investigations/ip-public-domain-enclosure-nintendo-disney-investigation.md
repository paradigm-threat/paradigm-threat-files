# Intellectual property enclosure — term extensions, nostalgia remakes, and whether the public domain is being prevented (open)

> **TL;DR:** **Assessment:** corporations have run a **public, repeated, successful campaign to postpone** the public domain. That campaign is the **statute and the trade deals**, not the Pixel Remaster. The last general United States term hike was the **Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act** (1998). *Steamboat Willie* still entered the US public domain on **1 Jan 2024**. A *Final Fantasy* reissue does **not** move the 1987 clock. The pre-*X* reissue chain (PlayStation load-time ports, then mobile redraws, then the Pixel Remaster) is public, repeated, and successful at **selling a substitute**. It is not a demonstration that the public-domain date moved. **AU-066.**

**Status:** Open. Author thesis first-class. Statutory timeline and the 1998 / 2022 / 2024 record are documented. “Never” and “the remake resets the right” are scored and rejected as legal claims.

## 2. Assessment

**Yes on the campaign. No on “ever.”**

The campaign is not a rumor and it does not need a leaked memo. It is on paper.

- Congress lengthened copyright in **1831, 1909, 1976, and 1998**, and the later two extensions **reached backward** onto works already created. Each hike landed as valuable works neared the public domain. *Eldred v. Ashcroft*, 537 U.S. 186 (2003), upheld the 1998 hike **7–2**. Justices **Stevens** and **Breyer** dissented. Breyer’s dissent is the judicial version of this file’s complaint: a very long term, applied to existing works, transfers value to heirs and assignees and gives the public almost nothing new in exchange.
- Disney’s role in 1998 is contemporary press, not folklore. The *Los Angeles Times* (18 Oct 1998) reported that Disney executives **led** the push for twenty more years because Mickey, Donald, and the rest were approaching expiry, and that chairman **Michael Eisner** took the matter to Senate Majority Leader **Trent Lott**. A Congressional Quarterly piece carried by CNN (10 Aug 1998) called extending Mickey’s copyright the company’s **highest priority** bill. Critics nicknamed the statute the **Mickey Mouse Protection Act**. The statute’s own name is the **Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act**, Public Law 105-298 (27 Oct 1998).
- The same political system has **kept exporting** the long term. The European Union harmonized to **life plus 70** in 1993 (Council Directive 93/98/EEC), and US lobbyists used that as the harmonization excuse. Canada, which had stayed at **life plus 50**, moved to **life plus 70** on **30 Dec 2022** because the **Canada–United States–Mexico Agreement** required it. Canada’s own consultation warned of a **twenty-year gap** in which **no** general-term works would enter the public domain. Japan lengthened its general term to life plus 70 effective **30 Dec 2018** in the Trans-Pacific Partnership implementation window. These are recent exceptions of the kind the author asked for: not a secret rider that names Mario, a trade obligation that moves the cliff for everyone.

**What failed, and why “never” is the wrong word:**

- No fifth general US term extension has been enacted since 1998. *Steamboat Willie* (1928), and the Mickey and Minnie **as depicted in that film**, entered the US public domain on **1 Jan 2024**. Duke’s Center for the Study of the Public Domain published the operating guide. Disney’s statement to the Associated Press, reported by *Fortune* (14 Dec 2023), said the association of Mickey with Disney “will not change” when that copyright expires, that **more modern versions remain unaffected**, and that the company will keep guarding against **consumer confusion**. That is a trademark and modern-copyright position. It is also an admission that the 1928 copyright ended.
- People used the expired icon immediately, including in the distasteful register the author separates from Santa and Lincoln. The cliff was delayed twenty years. It was not abolished.
- A remake, a port, or a “pixel remaster” **does not restart** the original’s term. [17 U.S.C. § 103(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/103) limits copyright in a derivative work to the **new material**. The 1987 *Final Fantasy* and the 1985 *Super Mario Bros.* keep their own clocks. The author’s fear that the remake **legally** prevents a later free remake is not how the statute works.

**What the stack still accomplishes, and why the author’s loss is real:**

The public-domain date on a corporate game is so far away that “after expiry, the audience remakes it” is a promise made to the **grandchildren of the customers**. Under the US **work-made-for-hire** rule, [17 U.S.C. § 302(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/302), the term is **95 years from publication** or **120 years from creation**, whichever is shorter. Applied to a 1985 publication, that is an entry date of **1 Jan 2081**. Applied to *Final Fantasy* (18 Dec 1987), **1 Jan 2083**. If a court instead treated a named living designer as the legal author, the term would be that person’s **life plus 70**, which can run **longer**. Either way, the people who paid in the 1980s do not live to see the competition the Constitution’s “limited Times” language was written to produce.

On top of that clock:

| Layer | What it does | Does it meet the author’s “loss of ability”? |
|-------|----------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Retroactive term extension (1976, 1998) | Moved a date the public had already been promised | **Yes.** The product was unchanged. The expiry moved. |
| 95-year corporate term | Puts Mario and the 1987 *Final Fantasy* out of legal competition until the 2080s | **Yes**, for any buyer now alive |
| New copyright on the remake only | Does not save the old work. Occupies the market with a **modern face** that stays locked after the original expires | **Partial.** The old code still expires. The face people recognize may be the remake. |
| Trademark, renewable while used | Stops uses that **confuse** buyers into thinking Disney or Nintendo is the source. Does not, by itself, forbid every story about a public-domain figure. *Dastar v. Twentieth Century Fox*, 539 U.S. 23 (2003), rejected using trademark as a **perpetual copyright**. | **Partial.** Real veto on branding and merchandise. Not a second copyright of infinite length. |
| DMCA § 1201 (Public Law 105-304, **28 Oct 1998**, the day after the term extension) | Bans **circumventing** a lock even when the use would otherwise be lawful | **Yes.** This is the layer that makes “I paid for the cartridge” fail against encryption. |
| Subscription classics | Access while the fee is current. Nintendo’s own FAQ: **no plans** to sell the Classics library outside Nintendo Switch Online | **Yes.** Stop paying, lose the games. |

**Pixel Remaster, scored against the trick thesis:** Square Enix’s series is a **new** copyright on redrawn sprites, interface, and rearranged music. It does not add a day to the 1987 term. It **does** match the political-economy half of the thesis. Square Enix reported **2 million** shipments and downloads by **2 May 2023**, **3 million** later in 2023, **5 million** by March 2025, and **6 million** by December 2025. Contemporary reviews praised convenience and the new scores and objected to the **missing** Game Boy Advance and PSP dungeons, jobs, and bonus episodes, to filtered sprites, and to a bad English font. The title says “pixel.” The sprites are not the original pixels. That gap is the author’s “what is being lost,” and it is visible without calling the product a criminal fraud. Fans who buy it have a legal way to feel finished. They have not received the 1987 code, the later bonus versions, or the right to build on either. The direct question — whether this product demonstrates a campaign to **postpone the public domain** — is answered **no** in §6.2. The postponement record is §4 and §5. The pre-*X* port chain is §6.1.

Nintendo does not need that one product to run the same pattern. It rents the old library through **Nintendo Switch Online** and, in March **2024**, took a **$2.4 million** settlement that shut down the **Yuzu** Switch emulator and, with it, support for **Citra**. Official nostalgia and tool-suppression run **together**. The remake did not retire the lawsuit.

**Information control, scored:** exclusive rights in derivative works ([17 U.S.C. § 106(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/106)) are exactly a right to decide **what the icon is allowed to say**. Parody is a defense, not a permission. Disney could put a boy through Hitler Youth indoctrination in *Education for Death* (released **15 Jan 1943**) and put Donald Duck in a Nazi factory in *Der Fuehrer’s Face* (released **1 Jan 1943**, Academy Award). Warner could send Daffy to Mars as Duck Dodgers (Chuck Jones, **1953**). A buyer cannot do the equivalent with Mario. Santa and Lincoln are the control case: the **person or the folk figure** is not a corporate work, so taste is the restraint, not a licensing desk.

**Consolidation, scored:** *Kingdom Hearts* and *Super Smash Bros.* do not extend anybody’s term by existing. They add **contracts** and **new depictions**. When one costume of Mickey becomes public domain, the Mickey the crossover audience knows, and Sora, remain locked, and every added rights holder has a veto. Fan anger does not itself amend the statute. The messy framework the author feels is the **license stack**. That stack is a real obstacle to “the icon, as people know it, belongs to the public.”

**LEGO, scored separately** because the 2026 specimen is not what a casual hearing suggests. The viral wartime clips are **LEGO-style AI videos**, not productions of the LEGO Group. Canadian Rapid Response Mechanism reporting links the operation (**Explosive Media**) to the Iranian state; the BBC interviewed a maker; wire coverage describes minifig Trump, Netanyahu, jets, and a classroom strike, with the message that Iran is resisting the United States. The author’s information-control point **holds**: a childhood toy-grammar carries a war message to people who recognize the bricks. The “only the corporation may speak” point **does not hold for this specimen**. A non-holder used a lookalike at very large scale. The LEGO Group’s rights did not stop it. Official crossovers (LEGO *Star Wars*, and the same logic as *Smash*) remain the consolidation case. The Iran videos are the **lookalike speech** case.

**Bottom line for the question asked:** there is an active campaign, conducted by statute, trade agreement, trademark maintenance, new derivative copyrights, and anti-circumvention lawsuits, to **keep the commercially valuable version out of public hands for as long as politics allows**. It has already moved the date by decades more than once. It has not yet written “never” into US copyright. The practical effect on a person who bought *Super Mario Bros.* or *Final Fantasy* in the 1980s is the same as never.

## 3. The clock

“Limited Times” is constitutional text, Article I, section 8, clause 8: Congress may secure exclusive rights to authors **for limited times** in order to promote progress. The first US Copyright Act (1790) took “limited” to mean **14 years**, renewable once for **14** more, **28** at the outside. The bargain was short on purpose. The author eats first. Then the work joins the same commons as a folk tale.

| Statute | Maximum for a typical published work | Applied to works already in existence? |
|---------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 1790 | 14 + 14 renewal = **28** | The starting rule |
| 1831 | 28 + 14 = **42** | Yes, forward onto existing copyrights in the pattern later courts accepted |
| 1909 | 28 + 28 = **56** | Yes |
| 1976 Act, effective 1 Jan 1978 | Named author: **life + 50**. Work for hire: **75** from publication. Pre-1978 works pushed toward **75** from publication | **Yes.** This is the first extension that swallows the modern corporate catalog |
| 1998 CTEA | Named author: **life + 70**. Work for hire: **95** from publication or **120** from creation, whichever ends first. Pre-1978 published works: **95** from publication | **Yes.** Twenty years added to copyrights **already running** |

A retail purchase has never been a purchase of the copyright. [17 U.S.C. § 202](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/202) says ownership of a copy is distinct from ownership of the copyright. The first-sale rule, § 109, lets you resell a **lawfully made copy**. It does not let you copy the work, adapt it, or break a digital lock. The author’s “I paid 100%” is morally about the **commons bargain** (exclusive rights must end). It is not a description of what a 1985 cartridge receipt conveyed even under the 1976 Act. The scandal documented here is that the **end date kept moving after the sale**, and that locks and subscriptions now take away uses the first-sale idea still pretends the buyer has.

**Works this file’s readers actually mean:**

| Work | Publication used for the arithmetic | US work-for-hire entry, if § 302(c) applies and Congress does not extend again |
|------|--------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Steamboat Willie* / 1928 Mickey | 1928 | **Arrived 1 Jan 2024** (95 years). Later Mickey films have their own dates. *Fantasia* (1940) is not in the 1928 grant. |
| *Super Mario Bros.* | 1985 | **1 Jan 2081** |
| *Final Fantasy* (first game) | 18 Dec 1987 | **1 Jan 2083** |
| Pixel Remaster series | Steam / mobile **2021–2022**; Switch and PlayStation 4 **2023** | Its **own** new expression: corporate term from **those** publication years, into the **2110s**. Does not move the 1987 date. |

Work-for-hire is the right default for a game made by employees and published by the company. It is a classification, not a fact this file has re-litigated title by title. A successful claim that a named individual is the author would start a **life-plus-70** clock instead, and that clock can expire **later** than 95 years from publication.

## 4. Disney’s recorded campaign, and the cliff that arrived

The 1998 extension is the cleanest specimen of an **active campaign**, because the beneficiary, the lobby, the bill, and the court case are all public.

Disney’s Mickey copyright under the 1976 Act was headed for expiry in **2003** (1928 + 75). Pluto, Goofy, and Donald sat a few years behind, which is the schedule the *Los Angeles Times* printed from Disney’s own spokesman. The fix was twenty more years, matched to the EU life-plus-70 term, so the 1928 film would last until the end of **2023**.

*Eldred* was brought by people whose business was the public domain. The Supreme Court held that “limited Times” does not forbid Congress from **extending existing terms**, so long as each extension is still finite. That holding is why a future fifth extension would arrive at the Court with a favorable precedent. It is also why 2024 matters: the political system **could** have bought another twenty years before New Year’s Day 2024, and it did not. The campaign is real, repeatable, and not automatic.

Duke’s guide is the practical correction to “trademark means Mickey never entered the public domain.” Trademark forbids uses **likely to confuse** someone into thinking Disney made or sponsored the new work. It does not forbid a clearly labeled new story that uses the **1928** mouse. Disney’s AP statement draws the same line from the other side: modern Mickey stays enforced; the company will chase **confusion**. Both sentences can be true. The author’s “never” is what the **modern costume plus the brand** still feel like. The 1928 drawing is the part the campaign failed to hold.

## 5. Exceptions and prolonging tools since 1998

These are the post-CTEA moves that match “exceptions made in recent times.”

**Canada, 30 Dec 2022.** Order in council brought in the Budget Implementation Act, 2022 amendments. General term became life of the author, the rest of the year of death, and **70 years**. The government said it was fulfilling CUSMA. Transitional text did **not** revive works already in the public domain before that day. The damage is the hole in front of the cliff: works that would have expired in the 2020s and 2030s under life-plus-50 now wait. Libraries told the consultation this would starve researchers and new creators, especially of **orphan** works whose owners cannot be found. That is a mass loss of ability with no new authorship purchased.

**Japan, 30 Dec 2018.** The Agency for Cultural Affairs states that the general term moved from life plus 50 to life plus 70 when the CPTPP entered into force in Japan that day. Works already in the public domain were not revived. Cinematographic works were already at 70 years from publication and were not the piece being lengthened. Same direction as Canada: harmonize **up**.

**European Union, 1993, still the template.** Life plus 70 was the number Washington was told it had to match. Harmonization has been a one-way ratchet.

**Trademark maintenance.** Registrations are renewed on use, in ten-year blocks in the US, with no life-of-the-author sunset. This is the only layer in the stack that is **designed** not to expire. *Dastar* (2003) is the limit: once the copied work itself is public domain, the Lanham Act cannot be turned into a requirement that restores copyright. Source confusion remains actionable. For a character the public meets as a **logo on a product**, that remaining action is enough to scare ordinary publishers. It is not enough to erase the 1928 film.

**DMCA anti-circumvention.** Passed as its own public law the day after the term extension. Section 1201 targets the **tools**, not only the copies. Emulation of an old console can be lawful engineering (the earlier Sony fights over Connectix and Bleem are the usual citations for clean-room emulators). Shipping or using a tool that **defeats a game’s encryption** is what Nintendo pleaded against Yuzu. The author’s “those zones should have become legal after a certain time” collides with this statute first: 1201 does not mature into permission when a term gets short. It sits beside the term.

**No general US term hike since 1998** belongs on this list as a negative finding. Proposals to **shorten** the term, including Lawrence Lessig’s public-domain-enhancement idea from the *Eldred* era, also failed. The live prolonging tools are trade deals, trademarks, derivatives, and 1201, not a new Mickey Mouse bill every Congress.

## 6. Remakes, and the Pixel Remaster

**Legal:** the original keeps its publication date. The remake is a derivative. Its copyright covers the new art, the new recording, the new code. When the original enters the public domain, anyone may copy **that** original. They may not copy the remake’s new expression. They also still have to avoid trademark confusion if they brand a product as if the company made it.

**Market:** the remake is what the store promotes, what video platforms amplify, and often the only **legal** way to play. Nintendo’s Classics FAQ says there are **no plans** to sell those subscription versions outside the membership. Square’s Pixel Remasters are sold as the faithful pixel return. Buyers who want the 1987 cartridge image, the GBA bonus dungeons, or a completed fan restoration are pointed at a store page that is none of those things.

**Quality, without the insult doing the work of a citation:** VG247 and other launch coverage said the first Pixel Remasters follow the **Famicom / NES** games and **omit** the extra dungeons and the *Final Fantasy II* post-game episode from the Game Boy Advance releases. Metacritic-side summaries credit the arranged soundtracks and call the picture a loss of detail. User-score bombing in 2021 was partly a protest that the games were **not on consoles yet**, which is a distribution complaint, not a review of the sprites. By May 2023 the series was on Switch and PlayStation 4 and past two million units. By December 2025 Square Enix’s own notice put the six-game total past **six million** (package shipments plus downloads).

That sales figure supports “nostalgic virtue moves a lot of copies.” It does not support “the games are so empty that the sales are a fraud.” The author’s grade — low, shoddy, a name that flatters pixels the product does not preserve — is a **critical judgment** aimed at the gap between the word *pixel* and a filtered redraw, and at the decision to ship **less content** than a 2000s handheld port while marketing the result as the classic. The judgment stands as Author. The gap itself is Documented.

**Author, Pixel Remaster as nostalgia exploit (2026-10-03):** the title is the tell. An upgrade that is less pixelized than the cartridge has no honest reason to keep the word pixel except leftover blocks sold as nostalgic pixel value. The older generation treated pixels as a hardware stage that would keep being upgraded. What shipped instead was years of parked pixel games and HD remakes that were never evolutionary or organic. The TLC contrast is Team Ninja’s 3D *Ninja Gaiden* on Xbox (2004), *Ninja Gaiden Black* (2005), and the later PlayStation editions — successive work on the same game, not a costume. Square upgrades closed original code the public never received, on a proprietary remaster engine the public also does not receive unless it leaks. Marketing is the pixel nostalgic experience at a full game price for an incomplete remake (GBA/PSP extras omitted is Documented). That is nostalgic virtue signaling. Penny Arcade’s drought-reset is the same function on the commentator side, not a payroll finding.

### 6.2. Does the Pixel Remaster demonstrate a campaign to postpone the public domain?

**No.**

Public: yes. Repeated: the chain above is the repetition, and the Pixel Remaster is one link, not the campaign. Successful: yes at **selling copies** (six million across the six games by December 2025) and at giving fans a legal current version so the argument can cool. Successful at **postponing the public domain**: no evidence in this product.

A postponement campaign has to move, or try to move, the **date on which the old work becomes free**. Disney’s 1998 lobbying did that. Canada’s 2022 trade amendment did that. Shipping *Final Fantasy VI* again in 1999, 2006, 2014, and 2022 did not. Each of those editions has its **own** copyright in whatever was new. The 2 Apr 1994 game keeps 2 Apr 1994. Under the US work-for-hire arithmetic in §3, that still points at **1 Jan 2090** for *VI* (1994 + 95, entering the following 1 January), and at **1 Jan 2083** for the 1987 game. The Pixel Remaster’s new art runs from 2021. It does not add a day to 1994.

What the chain **does** demonstrate, and what the author’s “unforgivable” is aimed at:

- The legally sold object gets **worse in a specific way** and people still buy the name. *Anthology* adds load times the Super NES did not have. RPGamer called the port moderately abhorrent and still described *V* and *VI* as strong enough to survive it. That is “usually well-received” sitting on top of a real loss.
- *I–III* draw less of this fury because the comparison class is smaller. *II* and *III* had no US cartridge. *III*’s first Western edition was already a different 3D game. *IV–VI* are argued against a Super NES original a lot of players still have in memory. *V* is the ugly middle case: no US cartridge, so *Anthology* was the first legal copy, and it was already the slow one.
- The Pixel Remaster is closer to a correction of the **2013–2015** mobile faces than to a plot to move a copyright date. It still is not the cartridge, and it still omits GBA bonus episodes. Fans who buy it are not thereby given the 1994 code or the right to compete with it.

The repeated act is real. The thing it repeats is **a new SKU**, not a new term. Calling that SKU the public-domain campaign would credit a store page with what only a statute has done.

**Does this prevent an emulation public-domain revolution?**

It prevents the **present-tense** version. A legal, competing, free remake of *Final Fantasy* made from the expired code cannot exist in the United States until the 2080s. Until then, a six-million-copy official remaster plus platform takedowns makes the audience feel supplied. The revolution the author wants was supposed to be **the law’s own second act**, not a pirate exception. The remaster occupies the years in which that second act should already have begun, if the term were still the 1790 or even the 1909 length. It does not repeal the second act. It postpones the audience for it until the audience is dead.

*Final Fantasy VII Remake* (and the later Rebuild entries) is a different specimen in the sibling dossier [AU-055](final-fantasy-vii-nintendo-story-sabotage-investigation.md): a story rewrite, not a pixel reissue. Same copyright structure. New work, old work’s clock untouched, cultural memory steered onto the new work.

### 6.3. Why remake a work whose copyright does not need it

**The clock does not require a remake.** US copyright has no use requirement. A game Square Enix never ports again stays copyrighted until the term ends. Trademark is the layer that can die of neglect: three years of nonuse is prima facie abandonment under the Lanham Act. *Final Fantasy* as a brand does not depend on a pixel reissue of *VI*. Mainline sequels, merchandise, and the word mark already supply commercial use. So “they had to remake it or the copyright would lapse” is not the law.

**They were expected to, and the expectation is not a statute.** After the PlayStation era, an anniversary re-release became a calendar product: the 20th-anniversary PSP editions, the 35th-anniversary Pixel Remaster. Fans, stores, and the games press treat “you cannot buy this on a current machine” as neglect. Square has been punished in public for both failures: years with no US *V*, and then a US *V* that loads for ten seconds. The audience demands a current SKU. The company can meet that demand with a SKU that is worse than the cartridge and still be said to have done the expected thing. Expectation and strategy line up. The fans ask for availability. Availability is also the fact the company needs.

**Absence would not repeal the copyright.** No US doctrine says “you failed to remake it, therefore it is in the public domain.” Orphan-works proposals are about owners who cannot be found. Square Enix can be found. A silent catalog is still a owned catalog. Copyright misuse is a narrow defense in infringement cases. It has not been used to strike a term.

**Absence would still be a political and a narrow legal problem, and that is the strategic point.**

The one place current US law attaches a consequence to commercial silence is the anti-circumvention exemption, not the copyright term. The Copyright Office’s 2024 rule, [89 FR 85437](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/10/28/2024-24563/exemption-to-prohibition-on-circumvention-of-copyright-protection-systems-for-access-control) (28 Oct 2024), lets an eligible library, archive, or museum circumvent a lock to keep a **lawfully acquired** video game playable **only if** the game is **no longer reasonably available in the commercial marketplace**, and only on the institution’s physical premises, with no distribution off those premises. A Pixel Remaster on Steam, or a Super NES game inside Nintendo Switch Online, is the fact a rights holder uses to say the game **is** reasonably available. That fact shuts the exemption for that title. It does not have to be the cartridge. It has to be for sale.

That exemption never legalizes a fan emulator. Even a total absence of remakes would leave ordinary players under the same circumvention ban. What absence would do is hand libraries a narrower door, and hand everyone else a photograph: **locked, and not for sale.** That photograph is what shows up in congressional hearings, in the next term-extension fight, and in the triennial 1201 proceeding. The 1998 case for a longer term included the claim that a longer term encourages continued publication of old works. Breyer’s *Eldred* dissent answered that most of the extended works were not being published. A company that will not sell *Final Fantasy VI* and will still sue the tools that play it makes Breyer’s photograph current. A company that sells a filtered redraw can say the system is working: the old game is on a store page.

**The strategy, with money set to one side:**

| Move | What it accomplishes | What it does not need |
|------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Keep the statute long | The actual postponement of the public domain | A remake |
| Ship any current edition, including a bad one | Answers “where do I buy this?” so the abandonment photograph cannot be taken. Feeds the “reasonably available” line in the preservation rule | Fidelity to the cartridge |
| Prefer a subscription when possible | Availability that ends when the payment ends. No durable copy left in the buyer’s hands | A remaster at all. Nintendo Classics is this move |
| Sue the tools | The unofficial version is often the better one (*Anthology* loads; the cartridge dump does not). Enforcement stops the better copy from becoming the normal way to play | A good official port |
| Let fans argue about sprite quality | The argument concedes the frame that the official edition is the one that matters | Winning the argument |

A low-grade remake is strategically sufficient. It is expected by the audience, it is cheap relative to a new game, it occupies the only legal storefront, and it denies opponents the clean case that the work was abandoned under a ninety-five-year lock. The absence the author describes would not by itself put *VI* in the public domain. It would make the lock harder to defend, in Congress and in the preservation proceeding, because the company would be asking the state to police access to a product it refuses to sell. The remakes exist so that request never has to be made in that form.

## 7. Paid in full, and the use still withheld

Nintendo Switch Online is the cleanest current match to “paid, and still not able to use it as your own.”

The publisher’s support pages state that NES, Super NES, and Game Boy classics (and the Expansion Pack systems) are available **while the membership is active**, and that there are **no plans to make those games available outside** the service. Save data can sit on the console through a lapse. The games do not. This is not the 1790 bargain and it is not even a 1990s cartridge sale. It is a rental of the company’s own back catalog, priced as a subscription, with the emulator and the ROM kept inside the garden.

The Yuzu settlement shows the other hand. In March 2024 Tropic Haze agreed to pay Nintendo **$2.4 million**, to stop distributing Yuzu, to hand over the project domain, and to accept a permanent injunction aimed at circumventing Nintendo’s technological measures. The Verge reported that Citra, the 3DS emulator from the same developers, went down with it. The case was pleaded as **circumvention and piracy facilitation**, not as “your copyright on *Super Mario Bros.* is about to expire.” The games inside that fight have decades left. The lawsuit still does what the author fears: it raises the cost of the tools that would let buyers run what they already own, and of the tools a future public-domain practice would be built from.

Fan-game takedowns (AM2R, *Pokémon* Uranium, and the long DMCA practice against ROM sites) are the same policy at the hobby layer. They are not re-documented fact by fact in this pass. They are the pattern around Yuzu: the audience’s own remake is treated as the enemy of the official remake, **during** the term, which is when a short term would already have made the audience’s remake lawful.

That mismatch is the information-control claim in plain clothes: recognition is a kind of power, and only some of the faces are in the commons.

Donald Duck in *Der Fuehrer’s Face* and Daffy Duck as Duck Dodgers are not evidence that the public accepts wartime or outer-space misuse of **any** famous figure. They are evidence that the **holder** may do it, and that the audience is trained to call that use legitimate because the holder did it. The same audience is trained to call a stranger’s use theft.

## 9. War messaging and the LEGO-style clips

**Disney, 1943, Documented.** *Education for Death: The Making of the Nazi* (director Clyde Geronimi, released 15 Jan 1943) follows a German boy, Hans, from picture book to Hitler Youth schooling to a march that ends in graves. D23’s catalog describes the short that way. *Der Fuehrer’s Face* (director Jack Kinney, released 1 Jan 1943) puts Donald Duck in a munitions nightmare in “Nutzi Land” and won the Academy Award for animated short. The Walt Disney Family Museum’s account records the studio’s wartime turn after December 1941, including training and propaganda films. These are the holder speaking **with the icons**, in favor of the US war and against Nazi Germany. Nobody licensed that speech from the public. The public could not have made it with those characters.

**Iran war, 2026, Documented as a lookalike operation, not as a LEGO Group film.** From March 2026, English-language generative videos in a minifig style spread under the name Explosive Media. The Canadian government’s Rapid Response Mechanism tied the operation to the Iranian state, estimated very large view counts in the first month of the US–Iran conflict, and noted Russian and Iranian state media boosting early clips. BBC reporting describes dying children, jets, and Donald Trump, with the through-line that Iran is resisting the United States. *The Straits Times* / AFP described a state-television brickfilm of Trump, Netanyahu, a classroom strike, and a closing line about pupils killed by American and Israeli attacks. The Verge described minifig political satire aimed at US and Israeli leadership.

Two readings, both kept:

- **Author, supported:** the brick grammar works **because children already know it**. A war message inherits trust and attention from a toy. That is icon power. Combining a familiar childhood system with a present war is the consolidation the author smells in *Smash*, practiced here by a state information operation rather than by a licensing summit.
- **Correction:** the LEGO Group did not author these videos. “LEGO animations made by the IP holder” overstates the record. The clips are **AI lookalikes**. They show that a famous plastic grammar can be **taken** for speech the company did not approve. That cuts against “the holder is the only one who can speak,” and it supports “the icon is the weapon, whoever holds the camera.”

The LEGO Group’s own long fight is the enclosure specimen for the **brick**, not for these videos. Functional patents on the stud expired; the Supreme Court of Canada in *Kirkbi AG v. Ritvik Holdings Inc.*, 2005 SCC 65, refused to let trademark law give the brick shape a perpetual monopoly after the patent, because the shape was functional. Minifigure sculptures and the LEGO word mark remain enforced. Official mashups (LEGO *Star Wars* and the rest of the licensed themes) are the corporate consolidation product: many locked universes, one plastic format, one company’s distribution.

## 10. *Kingdom Hearts*, *Smash*, and the messy framework

*Kingdom Hearts* (2002) is a Square–Disney contract product. *Super Smash Bros.* is Nintendo’s roster plus guests, each guest a separate license. Sora’s later arrival on the *Smash* roster stacks Nintendo, Square Enix, and Disney approvals on one character.

What this does **not** do: add years to *Steamboat Willie*, to *Final Fantasy VII*, or to *Mario*. Controversy on social networks is not a legal event.

What this **does**:

- Creates a new copyrighted depiction that becomes the picture in people’s heads.
- Multiplies the parties who must say yes before anyone else may use that picture.
- Gives each party a reason to keep its own trademark thick, because the crossover is a billboard for every brand in the room.
- Makes “the icon enters the public domain” feel like a technicality. The 1928 mouse can be free while the mouse standing next to Sora is not, and the audience meets the second one.

The author’s sequence — consolidation, then controversy, then a messier lock, then less chance of public ownership — matches the **contract and derivative** half. It does not match a claim that fan anger itself extended a term. The mess is the point of the structure: clearance stays expensive on purpose, because every added icon is another company’s veto.

A crossover with “no real political message” is still a demonstration that these faces can be gathered. The 1943 shorts and the 2026 brickfilms are what it looks like when someone **does** attach a message. The power to attach one, or to refuse everyone else the chance, is the same power.

## 13. Weak points

- “Scam” will be heard as an accusation of fraud. This file uses the author’s **loss-of-ability** definition and does not allege a false-advertising case.
- Pixel Remaster reception is **split**. Millions of buyers and several reviewers call the series the convenient way to play. The “shoddy” grade is the author’s, anchored by the missing handheld-era content and the filtered picture, not by a consensus that the games are unplayable.
- Trademark is easy to overstate. After *Dastar* and the Duke 2024 guide, “Mickey is trademarked so the public domain never happened” is false.
- The LEGO war thread is easy to overstate in the other direction. The videos are lookalikes.
- Work-for-hire dates are **arithmetic under a rule**, not a caption read off each game’s registration.
- Emulation law is fact-specific. This file does not map which emulators are lawful. It uses Yuzu’s **outcome** (shutdown, money, domain) as the enforcement specimen.
- The Morrison disposition is **unpublished**. Do not treat 56 F. App’x 782 as nationwide trademark doctrine. Do not allege the panel was an agent of Kyoto. “Foreign defeat” is AU-066g, not a poll.
