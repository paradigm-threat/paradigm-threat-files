# Minako Hamano and the Whitewash

The same rocket-barrel tune, two albums, two different names on the label

Video game music credits are not a receipt. They are a press release. During the Nintendo era especially, the name on the streaming app is often whoever survived the contract, whoever owned the IP, whoever the Western press already knew, or whoever got the supervisor line when the real composer was still in Kyoto typing hex into a sound driver. The problem did not end when CDs replaced cartridges. It just got easier to hide inside larger teams, live-session musicians, and “music recording” lines that sound technical until you realize the person who wrote the tune is not on the title at all.

This is a story about Minako Hamano, but it cannot stay inside her biography. Without the platform wars of the early 1990s, without the Philips settlement, without the Donkey Kong Country Returns and Tropical Freeze split, you cannot explain why a composer who co-wrote Ridley, the Wrecked Ship, and Mother Brain still disappears from the public story while men with supervisor titles absorb the rocket-barrel theme she may have been in the room for. Some of what follows is conjecture. One thread is not.

## The name that moves when the sequel ships

Open the Donkey Kong Country Returns original soundtrack on a modern player and find the rocket-barrel cue. The track lists **Kenji Yamamoto** as the composer. The theme is **Mine Menace** — the loud brass-heavy piece that plays when you ride the rocket barrel through Peaceful Pier and Crowded Cavern. The shorter version used in Gear Getaway appears on the Japanese Club Nintendo album as **Thrill! Gear Flight**.

Now open Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze and listen to **Rodent Ruckus**. Same lineage. The Mario Wiki documents it plainly as a European-styled arrangement of Mine Menace. The in-game music gallery uses the level name. The credit on Tropical Freeze goes to **David Wise**.

That is not interpretation. That is the official chain:

Returns names Yamamoto on the original rocket-barrel theme. Tropical Freeze names Wise on the arrangement of that same theme. Wise’s 2018 social post repeats that same division — Yamamoto composed the Returns rocket theme, Wise arranged the Tropical Freeze remixes — which is useful as **credit metadata**, not as proof he was in the room when the work was done. The Mario Wiki cites that post on its Mine Menace article.

So the reader can hold a concrete fact before any theory lands: **one musical identity, two credited authors, zero continuity in who gets called the composer when the franchise moves from Returns to Tropical Freeze.** If you only trust album metadata, Yamamoto owns the birth and Wise owns the rebirth. Hamano’s name is not on either line for that theme.

That is the whitewash in miniature — not a vague feeling about the industry, but a track you can queue up twice and watch the credit hop.

## What the streaming app hides

The correction matters because the fraud is **layered**.

Tropical Freeze has **four** rocket-barrel levels. Each one uses a **new arrangement** of the same Returns theme — **Mine Menace** — not a new composition from scratch. Fan-maintained credit tables that actually dig into the games list them as **Rodent Ruckus**, **Twilight Terror**, **Reckless Ride**, and **Blurry Flurry**. Mario Wiki documents all four as arrangements of Mine Menace. Wise’s 2018 post says Yamamoto “composed the amazing Rocket Barrel Theme for DKC:Returns” and that Wise “arranged the **remixes** for TF” — plural, matching those four level arrangements.

None of that is visible on a normal listen.

Open Apple Music, Spotify, or YouTube Music and the track listing **simply credits David Wise**. It does not say *arrangement*. It does not say *of Kenji Yamamoto’s Mine Menace from Returns*. It does not name Hamano. It does not explain that the “return of David Wise” marketing campaign is largely riding on **rearrangements of a Returns theme Wise did not write and was not present for**. A listener has to leave the player and go dig up wiki footnotes, fan credit tables, or Wise’s social posts to learn that his role on those cues was **remix duty on Kyoto’s prior work**, not fresh composition.

## The clarification that still erases her

Industry-friendly tellings praise Wise for posting to **clarify** authorship: Yamamoto wrote the Returns rocket-barrel theme; the core internal Wii team (including Hamano) deserves honor. That sounds like fairness. Author read: it is how the whitewash **ends**.

First the surface gives **Dave** the Tropical Freeze line and the “legend returns” poster. Then Dave — still the **narrator** — hands the Returns original to **Kenji**. Hamano appears only inside the **team** being thanked, never as the **named composer** of Mine Menace. Composition nouns go to Wise or Yamamoto. Hamano gets the support noun. Same ceiling every time: Sound Team–class billing with better PR.

His 2018 post is useful **metadata** (arrangement vs composition on TF). It is not a confession that restores her. It locks a **Wise ↔ Yamamoto binary** and leaves her where “Nintendo Sound Team” always leaves people — present, unnamed as author.

That is a slippery system by design. The **surface** credit supports the press narrative: *legendary DKC composer returns*. The **fine print** — buried outside the product — admits he only rearranged four variations of a theme Yamamoto already owns on the Returns album. Somebody would literally have to go dig up the interviews to learn he only rearranged those songs and did not compose them. That is a very slippery way to ensure the **“return of Dave Wise.”** Returns itself already hid the same theme behind Yamamoto on streaming while Hamano was **music** on the same project. Tropical Freeze then puts Wise’s name on the sequel arrangements **without** printing “arrangement of Returns” on the label the public actually reads.

So the provable misattribution is not only *who* gets the name. It is **what the name is allowed to mean**. Composer on the stream. Arranger in the footnote. Stakeholder in the marketing photo. Same song family, three different stories depending how deep you are willing to dig.

## The style does not change — and that is the giveaway

Listen to **Mine Menace** on Returns, then queue all four Tropical Freeze rocket-barrel arrangements back to back. The brass stabs land in the same places. The tempo pocket is the same. The harmonic language — the specific way the theme climbs and releases under barrel pressure — does not read like a new composer interpreting an old cue. It reads like **one hand** extended the same sketch four times with different orchestration and level-specific tweaks.

If David Wise actually wrote the Returns original **and** rearranged the Tropical Freeze versions, you are asked to believe something extraordinary: a man famous for the lush, rolling **Aquatic Ambience** and the jungle swing of **DK Island** can walk back into a franchise after a decade away, study a **Kenji Yamamoto** rocket-barrel theme he did not write, and **perfectly mimic** its timing, phrasing, and attack — four times — without leaving a fingerprint of his own SNES-era voice on any of them.

The simple explanation does not require genius. **He was not the original author, and he was not the rearranger.** The same Kyoto pipeline that produced Mine Menace on Returns produced the four variations on Tropical Freeze. Wise’s name is on the contract and the stream; the **style continuity** is what leaks the truth. Mimicry that perfect across four tracks is not a flex. It is a **continuity of authorship** the credit line refuses to print.

The complicated explanation — that Wise is a chameleon genius who can disappear into another composer’s idiom on command — is exactly the kind of story the whitewash needs. It turns a forensic observation into myth. Occam’s razor cuts the other way: **same style, same author, different name on the poster when the sequel needed a British legend.**

## The loop that sounds like Genie — and why it feels wrong

There is one moment in **Mine Menace** that has never sat right with me. Toward the **end of the song loop**, the brass and rhythm shift in a way that does not read like ordinary Nintendo house composition. It reads like **“Friend Like Me”** from Disney’s *Aladdin* — the Genie showpiece Robin Williams made iconic. Not a vague “both use brass” similarity. A **cover-like turn** in the loop tail that stands out hard if you know the Disney song.

I am not the only listener who heard it. On a December 2017 Patreon post for an acoustic cover of the Returns rocket-barrel theme, a commenter named **The HarDe One** asked: *“Am I the only one that hears ‘Friend Like Me’ from Aladdin whenever I hear this song?”* Another replied: *“Yo, Mash up that shit.”* ([jonnyatma Patreon / Kemono mirror](https://kemono.cr/patreon/user/331733/post/15833388), comments dated 2017-12-11.) Fan musician repertoires have since paired the two tracks side by side (e.g. [Wiggs Repertoire](https://sheet2site.com/api/v3/index.php?e=1&e=1&g=1&key=19TxyJnybnCWtZ69visj7KeoW1MyGfsWqJTCxy-FD9Hc)). **No official source** acknowledges an *Aladdin* reference. Nintendo’s composers — Yamamoto, Hamano, the R&D1 lineage — do not typically drop **recognizable Disney show-tune quotes** into action loops. If this is intentional, it is an anomaly. If it is accidental, it is an anomaly of a different kind. Either way it is **not** business as usual.

Then Tropical Freeze allegedly has Wise rearrange **four** variations on that same theme — the same theme that already carries this weird Genie echo — while streaming metadata credits him as composer and buries “arrangement” in footnotes. The stack gets stranger the longer you stare at it.

### Open speculation — Robin Williams, Genie erasure, and why I cannot un-hear it

**Label this section gut feeling, not proof.**

Robin Williams died **August 11, 2014**. *Tropical Freeze* shipped **February 2014** — so the four rocket-barrel arrangements were almost certainly locked before his death. I am not claiming the game was composed as a reaction to his passing. I am claiming something **cultural** may be threading through the same years: a pressure to **displace** Williams’s most iconic Genie moments and **reattach** them to something else first.

Williams had already fought Disney over **Genie exploitation** after *Aladdin* (1992) — contract breach, merchandising without consent, a lawsuit that shaped how his voice could be used for decades. After his death, estate restrictions on voice and likeness use were widely discussed; [Spotify Community threads](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Content-Questions/Aladdin-Sndtrk-missing-tracks/td-p/111658) from users who could no longer find **Friend Like Me** and **Prince Ali** on the original soundtrack reported the same frustration I felt: classic Genie songs **gone or hard to reach**, search results pushing the **2019 live-action Will Smith** versions instead. Tracks may return by region or album reissue — the point is the **discovery path**: a younger listener searching “Friend Like Me” can land on Smith before Williams even when the 1992 performance still exists somewhere in the catalog.

My read — speculative: someone may want a generation to **forget Robin Williams** as the Genie, or to encounter his defining showpiece **second**, through a replacement first. A Nintendo action loop that **quotes or mirrors** that song at its tail, then gets **four sequel variations** credited to a British stakeholder who was not on Returns, sits inside that same cultural weather even if no document connects the dots.

I do not know **who** put the Genie echo in Mine Menace, or **why**. I know it is unlike anything these composers usually do, that others have heard it too, and that the same era produced both **credit hops on this theme** and **Genie displacement on streaming shelves**. That is enough to keep the question open — not enough to close it as fact.

## Returns was the test, not the remix album you were sold

Donkey Kong Country Returns landed in 2010 with Retro Studios in Texas and Nintendo’s sound leadership back in Kyoto. Kenji Yamamoto was **music supervisor**. Minako Hamano was on the **music team** with Masaru Tajima, Shinji Ushiroda, and Daisuke Matsuoka. David Wise had already left Rare in 2009. He was not on Returns. Industry retrospectives later claimed he was too late to contribute if he wanted to — that is interview-era narrative. Returns shipped without him in the credits as composer.

Inside Nintendo, Shigeru Miyamoto and Satoru Iwata wanted the classic Donkey Kong Country sound preserved. Production reporting describes Returns as **mostly rearrangements** of material from the 1994 SNES game — the tunes the industry still labels “Wise” and Eveline Fischer on paper — plus new writing for the Tiki Tak Tribe. Returns was never marketed as “Wise is back.” It was marketed as Nintendo remembering how to make Donkey Kong feel like Donkey Kong, with Kyoto supervising Retro.

The Japanese Club Nintendo soundtrack CD tells a more granular story than the supervisor line. Track 3, **The Tiki Tribe Appears!**, lists **Minako Hamano** as composer and arranger. Track 8, **Sunset Coast**, same — Hamano composer, Hamano arranger. Those are not Wise covers. They are not SNES transcriptions. They are original Returns-era writing credited to Hamano on the album that Nintendo itself released to Club Nintendo members.

Track 20, **Thrill! Gear Flight**, is the Gear Getaway / Mine Menace family. On the fan-maintained soundtrack breakdown, composer and arranger are listed unknown on the table, but the Returns player metadata and Mario Wiki credit **Kenji Yamamoto** for Mine Menace. So even on Returns you already see the squeeze: Hamano gets named on some originals, Yamamoto gets named on the rocket-barrel theme that became the series’ most recognizable new action cue.

Returns was the experiment. All eyes were on whether Retro could revive the franchise. All eyes were on whether Nintendo’s internal sound lineage could still speak through a Texas studio. Hamano was R&D heritage in the room. Yamamoto was the Metroid partner she had already shared a score with on Super Metroid. The public got a great game and a warm soundtrack. The credit sheet already did not tell the whole story, but the game did not need a British legend on the poster yet.

## Vacuum inverse — why *Returns* had to exist and why it could not be clean

There is a principle for this gap. Call it **vacuum inverse** ([full doctrine](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/VACUUM_INVERSE.md)).

After *DKC3*, the silence was already normal for fans. They were used to the vacuum. The people who feel a vacuum **loudest** — and fight it **hardest** — are usually the ones who never publicly clash: stakeholders, original-artist faces, industry agents. They watch **pre-viral** questioning in controlled focused groups long before a thought goes mass-viral. They know when the question is forming: *why no more Country games — only DK64 and non-Country spinoffs?* By the time that question is asked out loud, a product is already queued.

Handlers understand that ignition in a vacuum can be a **tremendous explosion** they could lose. So *Returns* could not be allowed to prove such a success that the fan base would demand **Country forever**. The franchise’s common **roll** was forced onto **Wii Remote shake** — laggy, frustrating, anti-pattern — while other Wii games allowed Classic Controller-class button play that *Returns* withheld. *Returns 3D* did not fix the home-console problem. The serious button-standard edition — *Returns HD* (Switch, January 2025) — came roughly **fifteen years** later. Then, after *Tropical Freeze*, Country went quiet again. The public is still used to the vacuum. Demand never metastasized.

Why bother at all if Country was such an inconvenience? Why revive once, butcher the first playable form, wait a decade-plus for HD, then kill the line again? Under vacuum inverse, the answer is not “Nintendo forgot it sold.” The answer is **shaped detonation**: answer the murmur, cap the explosion, return to silence. The credit whitewash on the same cycle — Wise poster, Hamano demotion — is the soundtrack version of the same control.

And when *Returns HD* finally shipped (January 2025), Nintendo’s remaster credits **still would not print her name**. The original *Returns* composer and development teams — **Hamano included** — were wiped from individual credit under a controversial corporate policy and collapsed into “original development staff,” while Forever Entertainment sat front and center. **Kenji Yamamoto** still appears — not as music, but as a **Project Manager**. On the Wii game he was Music Supervisor. On the playable remaster he is management. Nintendo said it believes in “proper credit” and offered **no explanation**.

That asymmetry is the tell. Historians hear the *Returns* final boss and the *Metroid Prime* boss fights and say: same Yamamoto language, so he wrote both. This investigation says the opposite. The shared fight language is **Hamano’s** — she already owned Super Metroid’s boss book. She was the one willing to learn the new hardware and actually write the songs. Yamamoto held the **manager** seat. Prime’s paper credit without her name is the same whitewash as Mine Menace’s streaming tag. Outrage: **no playable console edition of *Returns* will ever carry her name.** His will.

## Tropical Freeze was when the name on the box had to change

Tropical Freeze was a different commercial problem. Returns had succeeded. Fans wanted a sequel with **new** music, not another round of “remember Aquatic Ambience.” Retro brought David Wise back. The VGMdb product credits for Tropical Freeze list **David Wise** under Music. Kenji Yamamoto is **music supervisor** again. Hamano is under **music recording**, grouped with engineers — not under the composer line.

Read that again. On Returns she was **music**. On Tropical Freeze she is **music recording**. The job title moved her from authorship to infrastructure in the official paperwork.

Wise’s return was the marketing story. Every interview was about the legend coming home. Hamano was in the credits, but not in the headline. The press built a narrative around Wise as if the entire Returns soundtrack had been his to begin with, when Returns had been largely Nintendo-internal supervision plus rearrangements of his SNES work — plus Hamano originals the album actually names.

Tropical Freeze then did something Returns did not do on the same scale. It mixed **live instruments** with the sequenced SNES-style language Returns had kept. Wise’s name sits on the whole primary soundtrack. The live layers sound different from the tight hex-programmed Rare era. Some listeners hear sloppier timing and arrangements built to showcase session musicians rather than the clockwork sample discipline of 1994. That is a judgment call about taste. The structural fact is simpler: **the credited composer changed at exactly the moment the franchise needed a visible “original composer returns” story.**

And here is where the provable Mine Menace chain matters again. Returns credited Yamamoto for the rocket-barrel original. Tropical Freeze credited Wise for Rodent Ruckus, an arrangement of that same original. Wise’s 2018 post describes the same split — useful as a credit receipt, not as proof of authorship. Tropical Freeze puts Wise’s name on a line Returns already assigned to Yamamoto, while Hamano, who was **music** on Returns, is **recording** on Freeze.

You do not need to believe every theory I hold to see the laundering pattern. **The same theme changes hands on the label when the sequel needs a different marketing face.**

## David Wise is not the author — he is the name on the contract

Here is the charge, stated flat.

**David Wise is not a composer, not a programmer, and not a musician in the sense that word is used for the people who actually built this music.** He is a **British IP holder** — a stakeholder and a public face — whose job in the story is to **receive credit** for work built by others. In the Japanese-side pipeline that matters for Returns and Tropical Freeze, the craft lives with supervisors and composers inside Nintendo’s lineage: Yamamoto, Hamano, Tajima, and the rest of the Kyoto team Retro depended on. Wise is the Western name the industry already knows from the Rare years, attached after the fact so the press has someone to photograph.

His long interviews — hex subroutines, assembly, typing waveforms into the SNES — are **part of the whitewash**, not evidence against the accusation. Of course the cover story gives the stakeholder a technical legend. That does not mean he invented or implemented the Donkey Kong Country sound. It means the myth was written completely enough that later journalists would repeat it without asking who was in the room when the cartridge shipped.

**Highlight — fraud in his own words / smoking gun.** Asked how SNES sound worked, he said they “could have used MIDI” but it would take “**processing and extra memory from the 64k**,” so he “continued to use HEX” ([Nintendojo](https://www.nintendojo.com/features/interviews/interview-david-wise)). That is **car-salesman doublespeak** (CPU ≠ ARAM; MIDI does not live in sample RAM; he did not design the Sony-class chip or write the driver). More important: the **story he still tells in 2011** is the **blocker** — *can we put MIDI on this?* — not how he mastered decades of chip craft. That is the fingerprint of a **MIDI-keyboard guy asking real programmers for MIDI support**, getting **no**, and recycling the refusal as expertise. He may have believed himself “composer” after throwing MIDI sketches at the team; without samples, instruments, tuning, and hex integration he does not earn the noun. He claims he typed the hex himself — the interview’s transaction suggests otherwise. **Performance artist / credit face.** The interviews do not merely fail to prove craft — they **expose** the act.

The **pro-MIDI / pro-industry** defense treats that MIDI layer and the finished commercial soundtrack as sacred museum material. With Rare’s blank NES receipts (~34+ titles without visible music credit), there is still **no contemporary public proof** that any MIDI sketch became the final player-heard chip data rather than engineer rewrite or invention. Working read: the IBM/MIDI setup was a **failed investment**, and the blank credits cover a UK staff that could not finish without Nintendo R&D. Detail: [dave-wise.md](dave-wise.md) § WORKING THEORY — IBM/MIDI was a failed investment.

On **Returns**, Wise was not on the project. The soundtrack was Nintendo-supervised rearrangement of legacy SNES themes **branded** as his on paper, plus **new originals** Hamano actually receives on the Club Nintendo album. Yamamoto holds supervisor credit and the rocket-barrel theme on streaming metadata. Wise had nothing to do with it except owning the Western copyright story the rearrangements point back to.

On **Tropical Freeze**, he was **brought back at the end** for the marketing arc: “the legend returns.” Product credits list him under **Music**. Hamano drops from **music** to **music recording**. He may have been present for **QA** — listening, commenting, approving — the way stakeholders are. His role in production could not be more minimized while his name on the poster could not be more maximized. The live-instrument layers on Freeze even sound unlike the clockwork programmed SNES work the myth assigns to him: sloppier timing, session-musician sheen, arrangements shaped to fit a limited public skill set rather than the hex-discipline the interviews describe.

The provable credit fraud on top of that: Tropical Freeze’s four rocket-barrel cues — **Rodent Ruckus**, **Twilight Terror**, **Reckless Ride**, **Blurry Flurry** — are all arrangements of **Mine Menace** from Returns. Returns credits **Yamamoto** for that original. Streaming services credit **Wise** on Tropical Freeze **without** printing “arrangement” on the line the public reads. Wise’s 2018 post is one of the only places that spells out arrangement-vs-composition; you will not get it from the track listing alone. He is billed like a returning author for remixing a theme Returns already assigned to someone else, on a project Hamano helped write while Wise was not on staff.

The industry needed a British name for the SNES golden age and kept using it when the real work moved back to Kyoto. Wise is not lying in interviews because he is confused. The interviews **are** the product.

## Hamano, prejudice, and why Returns was safe but Freeze was not

The next section is conjecture. I mark it that way because I cannot yet prove it in a court or a ROM header. It is still the emotional center of the investigation.

I do not believe Japanese women in this industry were treated fairly by Japanese men in general. Hamano joined R&D1 in **1991**, the same year the Sony CD rupture went public. She co-composed Super Metroid with Yamamoto while fans later said she was “overshadowed” and “often credited only to Kenji Yamamoto.” Shinesparkers ran a fiftieth birthday tribute full of fans saying she does not get the credit she deserves. That is not my imagination. That is the community reading the same gap I am reading.

My conjecture: Hamano endured prejudice and beat it with skill inside R&D, but **outside** Kyoto — in the Western press, in the Retro era, in the “Dave Wise returns” marketing — she could not prove herself to an audience that never learned her name. Returns let her participate as **music** while the story stayed about Retro proving itself and Nintendo protecting Wise’s legacy arrangements. That was not a threat to the whitewash. It was even useful. A magnificent understated soundtrack makes everyone happy, including critics, and keeps the supervisor and supervisor-adjacent names on the industry’s safe list.

Tropical Freeze was the threat. A sequel demanded **new** original writing at the level Returns had hinted at — Hamano originals on the Returns album, not just SNES remixes. If Hamano wrote the bulk of a new Freeze score and received composer credit, the press would have to acknowledge that the heart of Donkey Kong’s modern sound lived in a woman Nintendo’s own fans barely mention. That would crack the Wise return story and the Yamamoto supervisor story at the same time.

So the industry solution — my read — was to bring Wise back for the poster, move Hamano to **recording**, credit Wise for arrangements of themes that Returns had already assigned elsewhere, and let live musicians fill the sonic space with a different skill set that does not reproduce 1994’s programmed precision. Tropical Freeze becomes a recent whitewash of the same phenomenon as the 1991 CD wars: **control the name on the product even when the craft lineage points somewhere else.**

I believe Mine Menace should be examined with Hamano in the room, not assumed away because Yamamoto’s name is on the streaming tag. I do not yet have primary proof she wrote it. I have proof the tag moves between men while she is demoted from music to recording when the sequel money arrives.

## The bigger Nintendo arc this sits inside

Hamano is one composer. The surround is the case — and **this kind of theft was not normal**.

What happened to her is not the everyday sloppiness of game credits. It could **only** have occurred under the umbrella of the ridiculous situation Nintendo was put in when **Philips and Sony** — and eventually **Square** — treated Kyoto’s property as something to **take**, not something to **share fairly**. Routine under-crediting happens everywhere. **Strategic erasure of R&D1 craft authorship while IP is in flight** is a different crime, and it tracks the platform rupture year for year.

The media loves to say **Nintendo betrayed Sony first**. That is ridiculous. Nintendo wanted to **retain its IP** and its royalty model. Sony and Philips wanted **format control and licensing power** — which meant taking IP away from Kyoto’s toll booth. Same fight, inverted headline.

If you were alive for the **CD-i** era, you already know the visual proof. *Hotel Mario*. The CD-i *Zelda* games. Nintendo’s icons handed to another company **without R&D**, producing the shallowest, worst, most embarrassing version of the concept imaginable — barely games, really **interactive CD-ROM media** with Mario and Link silhouettes pasted on. Look it up if you need the cutscenes burned back into memory. That is what **IP ripped away** looks like when inventors are not in the room. Music credits are the same separation, just not funny enough for YouTube.

When **Final Fantasy** could literally be ripped away from Nintendo’s orbit — Square to PlayStation, announced January 1996 while *Super Mario RPG* smiled for the camera — it is not surprising that **Nintendo R&D credits** that never hardened into published track lines would be ripped away too. Composer authorship on products tied to contested IP becomes a **legal liability** under **IP lawfare**. General “music team” lines protect the company. Humble craft people who never demanded per-track billing — **Hamano reads that way to me**; fame gets you in trouble in Japanese studio culture — become easier to erase when the corporation is fighting for its life.

From 1988, Nintendo’s CD-ROM deals were never “just hardware.” They were fights over **who collected software royalties**. When those deals collapsed, Nintendo licensed Mario and Zelda to Philips for CD-i **without sending R&D with the characters**. Super Mario RPG was Square and Nintendo smiling while FF7 moved to PlayStation. The SNES CD platform died in 1993 when Nintendo pivoted to the N64. Secret of Mana and Chrono Trigger were designed for CD and cut down to cartridge. The incremental path — SNES plus CD plus coprocessor cartridges, the path Donkey Kong Country already proved — was abandoned for a hard reset into early polygon 3D. Two-dimensional craft did not die because it stopped working. It died because the industry chose a break **while IP was being extracted**.

Credit erasure fits that break. Rare hid credits on Battletoads to stop poaching. Nintendo later shipped apps without composer names. Super Metroid’s album split Hamano and Yamamoto fifty-fifty while fans remember only him. Returns and Tropical Freeze split Hamano from composer to recording while Wise’s name grows on arrangements of themes he did not originate on Returns.

None of that proves Hamano ghostwrote Final Fantasy VII. I do not claim that here. It proves the **incentive structure for real theft** — not accident — was already in place before anyone asked whether a specific tune was stolen.
