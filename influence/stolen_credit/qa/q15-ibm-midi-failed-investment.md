# Q15 — Did Rare’s IBM and MIDI setup produce the NES music?

**Question:** If David Wise and Rare had PCs, MIDI keyboards, and custom tools, doesn’t that prove they composed and compiled the NES soundtracks themselves?

## Answer

**Industry position:** Rare reverse-engineered the NES, built its own compilers, and typed music as hex. Chris Stamper, then programmers such as Dave Thomas and Mark Betteridge, wrote the drivers in Britain. Blank credits were a Stamper policy against headhunters. Hidden names in ROM hex prove the UK side compiled the final game. Different zero-page and math routines prove Nintendo R&D never supplied tools.

**Working theory (this site):** That stack is the cover. The IBM/MIDI spend was a **failed investment**. It did not produce finished console music. Wise auditioned MIDI. He was not a programmer. Stamper ordered tools. He did not code the catalog. The cues are game-music loops, not a MIDI composer’s song. MIDI would have produced **longer** pieces. An NSF of this music is about **1 MB**, so a 30-second cap is not a data excuse. On *Battletoads* the average musical loop is about 34 seconds; the longest main level theme (The Revolution) is about 58 seconds; the short active cues run about 24–30 seconds (Dark Calling, Clinger-Wingers, Wookie Hole). That looping craft is a tradition Wise did not have. Wise saying the SPC could not support MIDI means the MIDI path was tried and failed. Extra tracks, sound effects, drivers, and the retail compile still had to be done by people who knew the chip. **Author:** those people were Nintendo R&D. Rare reprogrammed around what R&D handed them. Blank credits hide that skill gap because the omission was profitable. A retail ROM cannot resurrect lost source, so “fingerprint” talk does not prove Kyoto was absent.
