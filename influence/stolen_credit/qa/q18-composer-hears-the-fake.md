# Q18 — How can a working composer hear that a credited composer did not write the score?

**Question:** Isn’t “it doesn’t sound like him” just taste? How can a real composer detect a fake one’s lack of ability?

## Answer

**Industry position:** Style arguments are fan opinion. Similarity across Rare soundtracks proves one house composer. Live-album roughness is session reality, not a fraud test. Outsiders should defer to the credit and the interview.

**This site:** A working composer is listening for craft limits, not for a vibe.

Someone who writes music hears when a cue was built to loop on a chip. The *Battletoads* cues do that: average loop about 34 seconds, longest main level theme about 58, short active cues about 24 to 30. A MIDI pianist hired from a shop does not arrive with that tradition, and a successful MIDI authoring path would have produced longer songs. The form gives the author away.

The same ear hears a player who cannot finish a take. On *Tropical Freeze* the live layers credited around David Wise have poor timing and weak soloing. A composer who had written the catalog the legend claims would keep going until the take was right. Unfinished playing plus an empty non-game discography is one person: a face, not the author of the clockwork tracks.

The ear also hears a later cue wearing an earlier game’s clothes. *Cannon Canyon* on *Tropical Freeze* sits next to *Battletoads* *Surf City*. The cover story says Wise did both eras. The public Tropical Freeze lines listed Minako Hamano and Kenji Yamamoto. A working conclusion is that the score was starting to sound like the “Wise” originals because the same chip tradition was still in the room, and the British name was brought back to catch it.

Taste is “I like this.” Detection is “this person cannot do the thing the credit says they did, and the thing on the cart is a different craft.”
