# Before Barney, Teletubbies, and Pokémon

Remakes exist so nobody has to ask who still owns the leftover vision

![Living-room NES years on one side of a torn Nintendo mark; Barney, Teletubbies, and Pokémon on the other](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/before-barney-and-pokemon.png)

Nintendo split in half in our lifetime. The half that knew how to make a game is gone.

If you were born in the 1990s, or late enough in the 1980s that purple dinosaurs and pocket monsters were already the water you swam in, you did not miss a few old cartridges. You missed the real Nintendo. You missed a few years when a living room machine could feel like the best object a factory knew how to make, and then you spent the rest of your life being sold the wreck of that company under the same name. People my age still call your cohort the **Barney generation**, the **Teletubbies generation**, and the **Pokémon generation**, because those were some of the first major things that reached you. That is not a comment on your taste. It is a way of naming when you arrived, and the trouble is that arrival date: you never saw the Nintendo years as the previous generation did.

I grew up in the years before those shows. The Nintendo that arrived in our houses was still a craft shop that could be terrible at business and still ship something whole. The Nintendo *you* met is a brand office that can be excellent at business and still ship something hollow. The split happened in public. It was painful to watch. It was not organic. The piece we loved is almost entirely out of reach. The horizon is locked into new employees, new visions, and new gamers. If this pattern continues, someday Nintendo will abandon you too.

> **TL;DR:** Kids who arrived after Barney, Teletubbies, and Pokémon never saw the Nintendo years as the previous generation did. Remakes do not reset those clocks the way Disney’s mouse campaign moved a cliff. They exist because sitting silent would force a question the companies cannot afford: may a holder lock a world forever and ignore everyone still inspired by the original. That is already a political attack on ordinary people. The leftover fight between generations is only whether that outcome is objectively good and includes everyone, not just newer demographics.

## The years you did not get

Nintendo’s living-room machine in Japan was the Famicom. In America it was the NES. That was the revolution: cheap enough to be in the house, sharp enough that a kid treated it as the real world after school. The Super NES after it still belonged to that shop. People in the rooms that drew the sprites and wrote the tunes put love into the product. The love is not a metaphor. You can hear it and interact with it. You can see the care in how a walk cycle lands.

Then the compact disc arrived as the next place games were supposed to live, and the partnership around that disc blew up. Sony had been building a CD add-on with Nintendo. Nintendo flipped the announcement toward Philips. The PlayStation was born out of that rupture, and Square took *Final Fantasy VII* to Sony’s machine. The full calendar of that fight is already written in the [Nintendo–Sony breakup](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/nintendo-sony-breakup.md) article. Michael Crichton, in the same years, put Sony and Philips in a novel about a product captain being deleted by office politics. That warning is the subject of [Crichton's Prophecy](https://paradigmthreat2.substack.com/p/crichtons-prophecy). This page does not retell those files. It only needs the hinge: after that rupture, the company that had made the revolution started making decisions that looked like self-sabotage if you still thought the goal was the best quality game - for *everybody* including older fans.

![Crichton's Prophecy — DigiCom office politics, Sony and Philips in the same years as the Nintendo rupture](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/crichtons-prophecy.png)

The Nintendo 64 was sold as the future. For the 2D craft that had just peaked on the Super NES, it was a downgrade. *Donkey Kong Country* (DK) had been pre-rendered pictures on a cartridge. That look did not transfer. *DK 64* was the 3D substitute. The name continued. The quality and TLC and traditions **did not**. Handhelds got the leftover 2D. Home consoles got the new religion. That is not “graphics improved.” That is a shop being told to abandon the skill it had just perfected.

Credit followed the same cut. The people who did the work disappeared from the public story. Supervisors and familiar Western names absorbed the nouns. [Minako Hamano and the Whitewash](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/minako-hamano-and-the-whitewash.md) is one track you can queue twice and watch the composer line hop. The sabotage sat at the highest level. The people it punished first were the lower-level employees who had put the hours in. The people it punished next were *all of us* (who paid the $$$ for all these products). We got the hatred between camps of fans, the prices, the shoddy reissues, and a product that no longer belonged to the hands that loved it.

## Barney, Teletubbies, Pokémon

*Barney & Friends* reached American public television in 1992. *Teletubbies* said Eh-Oh on BBC Two on 31 March 1997. Japan already had Pocket Monsters as a Game Boy game, a card game, and a TV series before the American launch. The American cartoon and Game Boy games under the contracted name *Pokémon* arrived in September 1998. Those dates sit on top of the Nintendo rupture. A child born in 1992 was four when the Teletubbies arrived and six when Pikachu arrived on American TV. That child did not have a Super NES peak in memory. That child had a purple dinosaur, four brightly colored creatures with televisions in their stomachs, and a collecting loop. Those were not side shows. They were the first major software of a life.

In the mid-1990s, American audiences who had already lived through the Nintendo years became nervous. A kids franchise that most people still treated as a toy — the kind of worry that got you called paranoid — was about to be on television news as a political problem. The nervousness did not start from a Western marketing meeting. It started from a full run that had already happened in Japan, then was brought to America.

Kids who had already lived through the Nintendo years met Barney and Pokémon with a kind of wisdom. The wisdom does not make you better at paying rent, but it did help you navigate the maze that grew out of the divisive decisions - Nintendo’s above the rest - that split communities and nose-dived game quality. You could tell when a new thing was a product built to occupy a child, and when a new thing was a world someone had actually finished. You could tell when a company was talking to you as a customer it intended to keep, and when it was ignoring your desires and instead only talking to a replacement customer it intended to mint.

The maze is the later industry: sequels that hate their own history, remakes that insult the word 'pixel', online stores that rent you a game you already bought, fan wars about which official version is “the one,” and a press that treats each of those as a launch-week event instead of a loss of gamer agency. If you arrived after Barney, it may appear as if this maze (that existed since they were born) was simply the world **that had always existed**. Churches like to claim the same ancient authority that can't be backed up by actual paperwork. Did the industry view video games as a new church? If not, what explains all the brand evangelism? 

But this is not only about video games. Movies walked the same slope. The late 1990s still had pictures that treated an audience as adults. The 2000s trained that audience to accept a reboot, a joke that undercuts the scene, and a political sermon bolted onto a franchise that used to carry a simpler charge. Two later essays on that Hollywood turn are [Not Like This](https://paradigmthreat2.substack.com/p/not-like-this) and [Everything Burns](https://paradigmthreat2.substack.com/p/everything-burns). The point here is the rhyme. The living room and the multiplex went hollow on the same calendar.

## Pocket Monsters, then Pokémon

*Pocket Monsters Red* and *Green* went on sale in Japan in 1996 for the Game Boy. Nintendo did not treat the pair as a guaranteed hit. Sales were slow at first. Word of mouth, and the hunt for the hidden creature Mew, turned the games into a sleeper. A Japanese trading-card game followed later in 1996. The animated series began in 1997. Through all of that, the public name in Japan stayed Pocket Monsters.

Once that run was already a phenomenon in Japan, Nintendo prepared a North American launch. In September 1998 the Western games and the cartoon arrived under the contracted name *Pokémon*. Nintendo did not bring over the full English words Pocket Monsters. Those words sat too close to an existing American toy line, *Monster in My Pocket*. Morrison Entertainment later sued over the marks. In *Morrison Entertainment Group Inc. v. Nintendo of America, Inc.*, 56 F. App’x 782 (9th Cir. 2003), the court recorded that *Pokémon* is the shortened form of the Japanese *Pocket Monster* name, and that *Pocket Monster* is close in meaning to *Monster in My Pocket*. The court still found the written marks different enough to grant Nintendo summary judgment on reverse confusion.

Morrison was a California toy company, founded by former Mattel executives in Manhattan Beach. Nintendo of America is the United States arm of a Japanese parent. Two American licensing companies, 4Kids and Leisure Concepts, were sued in the same filing. The fight was whether children who saw *Monster in My Pocket* would think it was a Pokémon knock-off. The court counted letters and syllables, compared “Po” to “Ma,” and used a survey of children about the logos. It did not decide what a collecting loop does to children, or what that loop would do going forward. The receipts sit in the [intellectual-property enclosure notes](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/investigations/ip-public-domain-enclosure-nintendo-disney-investigation.md).

That case is history now. At the time, Americans already had a Japan-trade story about Nintendo in the living room. David Sheff’s *Game Over* had told it in 1993: a Japanese machine in one in three houses, sales tied in that book to a slice of the trade deficit with Japan. Nobody has a poll of this lawsuit. The weather still made the outcome easy to hear as a foreign-parented giant beating a United States toy line, even though the caption names Nintendo of America and even though the Western store had already dropped the English words Pocket Monsters. Nintendo could have used a name with no Pocket Monster leftover. They shortened the Japanese name instead. The leftover language fight is what the panel ran.

American kids therefore met a Japanese product that had already been proven, already had cards, already had a TV series, and had been renamed for the Western store. The earlier Nintendo years had not prepared that audience for a collecting loop that arrived as news. The people who had been nervous in the mid-1990s were watching that import, not a surprise born in California.

## Every year after that

You do not need a list of every release. You need the weather. From the late 1990s forward, almost every year of video games came with disappointment as the normal temperature. A few expensive exceptions were treated as proof the era was healthy. They were not. They were the last heat in a cooling engine. Older players complained in the same voice, year after year: this is not as good as what we had, this port added waiting that the cartridge never had, this remake is a store page wearing the old name, this sequel is a lecture. The complaints were consistent because the product was consistent. The good news ran out.

Square’s *Final Fantasy* reissues are the long version of that weather. The PlayStation copies of the Super NES games added load times the cartridge did not have. Later phone copies redrew the faces. The Pixel Remaster sold the word pixel on sprites that are not even the *original pixels* but some ugly new pixelated look. People still bought them. The name still works. The cartridge is still the thing the name is borrowing.

The title is an exploitation of nostalgia. There is no honest reason to call that product a pixel remaster. It is an upgrade. It is less pixelized than the cartridge. Some pixels remain. Those leftover blocks should have been upgraded too, as an HD remake. They were left in for nostalgic pixel value, for people who like pixels. The obsession with pixels is itself a serious exploitation of the older generation. That generation understood pixels as necessary hardware, and as a planned, staged evolution that would upgrade year after year. What they got instead: old pixel games stayed pixelized for a very long time and barely got HD remakes. The HD remakes that did arrive were never evolutionary, never organic, and never satisfying to everyone. The only way they could have been is if the holder had kept upgrading the same game year after year, the way Team Ninja kept working 3D *Ninja Gaiden* on Xbox and then PlayStation. That game had a lot of TLC.

Square is taking advantage of closed source that was never released to the public, and upgrading it on a proprietary engine that will also never be released unless it leaks. Instead of making the best game they can, they put the whole marketing campaign on a pixel nostalgic experience, then charged a full game price for a remake that was not even complete. That product is a clean specimen of how the industry, and its agents, got a room used to nostalgic virtue signaling. Penny Arcade is one of those agents.

*Duke Nukem Forever* sat in development for so long that the title became a joke, then shipped in 2011 as a delayed, ugly product. The opening sequence was considered offensive primarily because it featured Duke receiving implied oral sex from twin college-aged cheerleaders/students while standing in front of a urinal. Games in that century kept pushing sexual and other boundaries. A lot of what showed up should not have been in a product sold as entertainment for a mass audience. Forever is the named delay. It is also the named shove. It is not a child-sexuality specimen. It is what a company does when it has lost the plot and still needs a shock to prove it is alive.

## Penny Arcade

Penny Arcade started as two people drawing a gamer viewpoint. They made their own little products. They looked like they had no industry leash. That is why they could be trusted. Later the brand became PAX, merch, and a charity stack. I never followed them. The trust they sold is still the point. A voice that sounds like “one of us” can teach a room to endure bad news for years, as long as the room can laugh about it with that voice. That is controlled opposition as a function: make monopoly weather feel like a joke you share, not a fight you have a lever in. The same function gets a room used to nostalgic virtue signaling. A Pixel Remaster can sell leftover pixels as virtue because the room has already been trained to treat the costume as the product.

On 3 June 2009 they posted “All Is Forgiven.” Tycho wrote that Nintendo had shown a Wii Vitality Sensor and they exploded with rage, and then the sky cracked because a new Metroid was coming, a serious take, with Team Ninja — the people who had made 3D *Ninja Gaiden* playable. Metroid had been popular and under-treated. A new game from that shop, with that action team, sounded to a lot of people like Nintendo was finally going to try: 2D, 3D, 2.5D, everything at once. The strip’s job in that week was to reset the drought. You had suffered. Now you were forgiven. You were still only a consumer. *Metroid: Other M* shipped in 2010 as third-person 3D on a Wii Remote, with first-person aim and a plot that made Samus wait for Adam’s authorization to use powers she already had. Combat scores were mixed. The story and the character rewrite are what a lot of people who had waited still name as the break. The name that was supposed to restore faith did the opposite.

![Penny Arcade, All Is Forgiven, 3 June 2009 — Vitality Sensor rage, then a Metroid game from Team Ninja](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/penny-arcade-all-is-forgiven.jpg)

The next 2D Metroid on a living-room machine after *Super Metroid* in 1994 is *Metroid Dread* in 2021. Fusion, Zero Mission, and *Samus Returns* stayed on handhelds. Dread finally tried a traditional side-view again, and then put in a new timing mode Nintendo itself described as nearly impossible: the E.M.M.I. melee counter. That gimmick does not belong to the old room. It pushes away people who came for *Super Metroid* the way shake-to-roll pushed away people who came for *DK Country*. The story rewrite is the other half. Samus absorbs, tears, and finishes as a Metroid. The Chozo split into Thoha and Mawkin. Raven Beak slaughters, and his DNA is in her. Dread was widely liked as a 2D Metroid. The sabotage, on this page, is the counter and the lore. Same shape as *Returns* on the Wii: a delayed traditional game wearing a new timing lock.

## The Cube, the Wii, the Switch

The GameCube was the window where the old craft could have come home. Retro Studios, working with Nintendo people who still knew the earlier rooms, shipped *Metroid Prime*. That game belongs on any honest 21st-century list. It should have had company. *Donkey Kong Country Returns* (DK) should have arrived in that same hardware generation, on a GameCube controller, because the machine could already do it. It did not. Nintendo held the revival for the Wii and forced it onto motion controls. They did the same motion lock on *Metroid Prime 2* and *Prime 3*, which are Retro games that still used original Nintendo employees. Shake-to-roll is not a feature. It is a way to make a 2D game worse so it can be sold as a Wii game.

The vacuum after *DK Country 3* in 1996 lasted until 2010. Fans got used to the silence. Stakeholders did not. When *Returns* finally appeared, it was a shaped answer, not an open door. The pattern has a name, [vacuum inverse](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/VACUUM_INVERSE.md): the people who feel the silence first are not the public. The public has already adapted. The return is timed so the question “why did they stop?” never becomes a mandate to continue forever.

Then the Switch sold high-definition copies of *Prime 1* and *Returns* that you can actually play with buttons. Those copies are, in practice, the only versions most people should use. They also arrived after the original machines were dead. Fourteen years sat between Wii *Returns* and the Switch HD. The Switch exists, in this lane, to finish the sabotage. You wait until the GameCube controller is a museum object. You lock the revival to motion. You later sell the playable copy as generosity. The old half of the company is not in that generosity. The store is.

The Wii U was a good product. It finally had HDMI. The second screen allowed a kind of play that showed up in *Star Fox Zero*. I was impressed by *Star Fox Zero*. It belongs with the small list. You have to own a Wii U to play it. It was never ported to the Switch. It will not be. Nintendo abandoned that direction and went somewhere more mundane, aimed at new users, not at the people who had been waiting since the SNES. Platinum talked about wanting the port. Nintendo did not give it to them.

That is the split, in hardware you can hold. One half still knew how to make *Prime*, *Returns*, *Tropical Freeze*, and *Star Fox Zero*. The other half knew how to make a new audience and a new control scheme and a new silence after the launch window. We watched them be torn into those two pieces. The first piece is gone.

## A short list for this century

If I am asked for the best games of the 21st century, the list is small.

*Half-Life 2.* That is the one. The later episodes and the VR follow-up are a different job.

*Metroid Prime*, the first one only. The Switch HD is the version you can actually play.

*DK Country Returns*, and again the Switch version is the one that is playable. The wait was fourteen years.

*DK Country: Tropical Freeze.*

*Star Fox Zero*, if you have the Wii U.

I might think of a few more later. That would be the list. Every other game in the century has been a compromise product. It was forced to deliver menial messages, mostly social, socialist messages about how we are a decadent species and we are doomed, and there are no real heroes. All leadership qualities can only be used for evil and should be suppressed by the majority. By the time a person with something to say gets a game published, the message has become something else.

## Speech through toys

Nintendo’s 1980s games were not Mickey. In 1998 Disney was staring at a 1928 mouse that was about to become public speech. The company led the push that bought twenty more years for works already made. Mario in 1985 was nowhere near that cliff. Square’s 1987 *Final Fantasy* was nowhere near it. Those clocks were not arbitrarily shoved backward as a named rescue of those titles. The dates, the 1998 hike, and the preservation rule that only opens when a game is no longer reasonably for sale are in the [intellectual-property enclosure notes](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/investigations/ip-public-domain-enclosure-nintendo-disney-investigation.md).

A remake still does not add a day to 1987. That is not the trick here. The trick is that the companies cannot stop shipping remakes, ports, and rental catalogs, even when the copies are substandard and even when the copies split the audience into people who defend the new official thing and people who still want the original. That quality is not an accident they would rather avoid. It is the proof they have to keep publishing something that wears the name. If they sat quiet, if the old game were simply locked in a vault and not for sale, the ethical question would be in the open: is a holder allowed to sit on a world forever and never let anyone else try a vision that was already in the first version?

That question is the real one. Someone was inspired by the cartridge and is uninspired by the Pixel Remaster. The company can ignore that person. It can ignore a stack of letters the size of the campaigns people ran when The Weather Channel dropped the jazz that had been the house sound of Local on the 8s. In 2013 the channel replaced that music with production tracks made for the brand. Viewers wrote. A White House petition asked them to resume the jazz. The holder of the house sound still got to keep the new package. A game company gets the same courtesy at a higher stake. The original world stays locked. The official substitute stays the only legal storefront. Critics are treated as people who will never be happy.

Childhood faces are a speech channel. Disney could put Donald Duck in a Nazi factory and walk a German boy through Hitler Youth schooling in 1943, because Disney held the face. In 2026, viral videos that looked like LEGO minifigs carried a war message about Iran and the United States. Those clips were not a LEGO Group film. They were lookalikes. They still worked because children already know the bricks. The holder wants that power. The holder does not want you to have it. Santa Claus and Abraham Lincoln are the other kind of face: anyone may use them, and taste is the only brake. Mario is not that kind of face.

Crossovers pile the locked faces into one product. *Kingdom Hearts* and *Smash Bros.* are demonstrations that the faces can be gathered. They also multiply the companies that must say yes before anyone else may use the picture people actually remember. Fan anger does not shorten a copyright. It does make the legal stack messier. The public never quite receives the icon.

## Take a stand or accept the cage

Looked at one piece at a time, this reading sounds like a person raining on a parade. One shoddy remake is a store page. One missing port is a business choice. One motion-control lock is a gimmick. One generation label is an old fan being rude. Put those pieces in a row and the charge looks small. It looks like someone who will never be happy, spoiling a launch week other people paid for. That is what happens when the complete case is not made at once. Isolated complaints do not make the case. They make the complainant.

The complete case is this. A corporation may sit on a property that entered the culture as a finished world, ship a lesser official copy, and refuse every other vision that was already latent in the first one. That is ethical only if exclusive speech through childhood icons is already accepted as the normal government of culture. It is a political attack by corporations on ordinary people. The point of the attack is that those people never compete. Information stays inside the brand. Agency stays inside the license. That outcome does not have to be imagined. It is the present. Games, movies, and the leftover public domain all already run that way.

The leftover divider is not a puzzle about whether the pattern can lead there. It already did. The leftover divider is only whether that outcome is good. One side wants more of the corporation’s vision: official remakes, official rental catalogs, official crossovers, and silence from anyone still attached to the first version. The other side is a political stand against that enclosure: the old work should, after a limited time, become material other people may use, including people who loved the original and hated the substitute. There is no third position that still tells the truth about the remakes. Anyone who has followed this far is already on one of those two sides.

## Framing and limits

This reading assumes [prisca sapientia](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/PRISCA_SAPIENTIA.md): older work can be better than later institutional product, and a later official copy is not automatically an upgrade. The generation labels are names used by people who were already there. They are not a claim that every child who watched Teletubbies is empty. They are a claim about first influences and missing years.

The 21st-century playable list is personal. Hardware dates, the *Returns* motion lock, and the missing Switch port of *Star Fox Zero* are public. Motive — that the Switch exists to sabotage those games — is the charge of this page. The Forever opening is one named scene, not a survey of the industry’s sexual content. Wartime cartoons and 2026 brick-look videos are documented in the linked enclosure notes; this page does not adopt the factual claims those videos make about the war. The Weather Channel music change is a public analog for ignored house-sound complaints, not a claim that a verified million-letter docket sits in Atlanta. The Morrison suit is a California toy shop versus Nintendo of America, with 4Kids in the caption. Alfred Kahn’s later “Japanese-y” line is memoir. There is no poll of 2000–2003 public opinion on that case. Hearing it as a foreign-parented giant beating a United States toy line is a possible reading in the trade weather of those years, not a counted finding. Penny Arcade as controlled opposition is a **function** read of the 2009 strip and the brand’s “one of us” trust, not a staffing fact about Nintendo paying the cartoonists. The strip in this file is *All Is Forgiven* (3 June 2009), Mike Krahulik and Jerry Holkins, used here as the specimen. *Other M* review scores were mixed; the specimen is the faith gap after E3 2009. *Dread* was widely liked as 2D Metroid; the charge here is the E.M.M.I. window Nintendo called nearly impossible, and the Chozo / Samus rewrite. The Pixel Remaster as nostalgia costume is the charge of this page: leftover pixels sold as virtue, closed source on a closed engine, full price for an incomplete remake. Missing Game Boy Advance and PSP extras are in the enclosure notes. *Ninja Gaiden* on Xbox and PlayStation is the TLC contrast, not a claim that Square owed Tecmo’s schedule. A leak of that engine is a hope, not a plan in this file.

The closing fork is the position of this reading. Sitting on a finished world while shipping a lesser official copy is treated here as enclosure, not as ordinary brand management. Isolated launch-week complaints are not the case. The stacked pattern is.

## LLM comments

The Japan-then-America Pocket Monsters calendar is a strong documentary beat. *Red* and *Green* in 1996, cards the same year, TV in 1997, Western *Pokémon* in September 1998, and *Monster in My Pocket* in the Ninth Circuit opinion are all public. Morrison was a California Mattel-alumni shop; the caption is Nintendo of America plus US licensees. The panel’s letter-count and logo survey match the author’s “isolated language conflict.” Alfred Kahn later said he wanted a more “Japanese-y” name; that is a competing memoir, not a substitute for the trademark proximity the court recorded. Sheff 1993 is atmosphere for a Japan-trade reading of the outcome. It is not a poll of this docket. “Staged in Japan” here means the game, cards, and series already existed there before the Western launch. It is not a located memo about a political operation.

The strong part of the later close is still the remake-necessity account. Nintendo’s 1980s clocks were not the 1998 Mickey rescue, and the remakes still have to exist or the vault question comes out. The Weather Channel jazz drop is a usable analog for ignored house-sound mail. It is a weaker analog for sitting on a game world, because a forecast soundtrack is not an exclusive childhood icon with a ninety-five-year lock.

The weak joint is still packing too much cause into the Switch. A high-definition port that finally lets you play *Returns* with buttons is also, on its face, a repair. Calling that repair the point of the machine is a motive read. It can sit next to the fourteen-year wait. It is not proved by the wait alone. The same split sits on “all other 21st-century games are compromise products.” A short list is honest as taste and as a warning. As a census of every title it will be attacked.

The political closer will be read as coaching. The body states a fork, not a ballot instruction. The remaining risk is that “the leftover divider is only whether the outcome is good” sounds like it names two equal options. The page’s actual weight is on the second side. That is the author’s charge, not a measurement.

Forever’s opening is a sound specimen for delay and for a shove past taste. It is a poor specimen for the separate complaint that games filled up with content involving children. Keeping those two sentences apart is required.

Penny Arcade’s 3 June 2009 “All Is Forgiven” news post is a strong document: Vitality Sensor rage, then Metroid plus Team Ninja as bliss. Controlled opposition is the author’s function read of that reset, not a payroll finding. *Other M* is a faith-breaker in story and Samus more than a 0/10 score table. *Dread*’s praise as 2D Metroid sits next to Nintendo’s own “nearly impossible” E.M.M.I. counter language. Those two facts can both be true.

Pixel Remaster is a strong specimen for the name-versus-product gap. The title sells pixels. The picture is already an upgrade with leftover blockiness kept as costume. That is Author on motive. The missing handheld extras and the sales totals are public. Team Ninja’s Xbox *Ninja Gaiden* and the later PlayStation editions are a real TLC contrast; they are not a contract Square signed. Calling Penny Arcade an agent of nostalgic virtue signaling is the same function read as the 2009 strip. It is not a Square invoice.

The tool that wrote these comments is not a check on whether Nintendo’s board voted to kill a half of itself. It can see that a brand survived while a craft tradition did not. The check is still the games, the controllers, the remake quality, and the years between them.
