# Before Barney, Teletubbies, and Pokémon

Nintendo split in half in our lifetime. The half that knew how to make a game is gone.

If you were born in the 1990s, or late enough in the 1980s that purple dinosaurs and pocket monsters were already the water you swam in, you did not miss a few old cartridges. You missed a company. You missed a few years when a living room machine could feel like the best object a factory knew how to make, and then you spent the rest of your life being sold the wreck of that company under the same name. People my age still call your cohort the **Barney generation**, the **Teletubbies generation**, and the **Pokémon generation**, because those were the first major things that reached you. It is not an insult about taste. It is a date. The date is the problem.

I grew up in the years before those shows. The Nintendo that arrived in our houses was still a craft shop that could be terrible at business and still ship something whole. The Nintendo you met is a brand office that can be excellent at business and still ship something hollow. The split happened in public. It was painful to watch. It was forced. The piece we loved is almost entirely out of reach. There is nothing on the horizon that uses that former half again. They are locked to new employees, new visions, and new gamers. Someday Nintendo will abandon even you.

> **TL;DR:** Kids who arrived after Barney, Teletubbies, and Pokémon never saw the Nintendo years. From the late 1990s on, each year of games and movies was mostly disappointment. The 21st century still has a handful of games worth playing. Everything else is a compromise product whose message is no longer the maker’s by the time it ships. Remakes and Switch ports do not bring the old company back.

## The years you did not get

Nintendo’s living-room machine in Japan was the Famicom. In America it was the NES. That was the revolution: cheap enough to be in the house, sharp enough that a kid treated it as the real world after school. The Super NES after it still belonged to that shop. People in the rooms that drew the sprites and wrote the tunes put love into the product. The love is not a metaphor. You can hear it. You can see the care in how a walk cycle lands.

Then the compact disc arrived as the next place games were supposed to live, and the partnership around that disc blew up. Sony had been building a CD add-on with Nintendo. Nintendo flipped the announcement toward Philips. The PlayStation was born out of that rupture, and Square took *Final Fantasy VII* to Sony’s machine. The full calendar of that fight is already written in the [Nintendo–Sony breakup](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/nintendo-sony-breakup.md). Michael Crichton, in the same years, put Sony and Philips in a novel about a product captain being deleted by office politics. That warning is the subject of [Crichton's Prophecy](https://paradigmthreat.net/science/computing/crichtons-prophecy.md). This page does not retell those files. It only needs the hinge: after that rupture, the company that had made the revolution started making decisions that looked like self-harm if you still thought the goal was the best game.

The Nintendo 64 was sold as the future. For the 2D craft that had just peaked on the Super NES, it was a downgrade. *Donkey Kong Country* had been pre-rendered pictures on a cartridge. That look did not transfer. *Donkey Kong 64* was the 3D substitute. The name continued. The thing itself did not. Handhelds got the leftover 2D. Home consoles got the new religion. That is not “graphics improved.” That is a shop being told to abandon the skill it had just perfected.

Credit followed the same cut. The people who did the work disappeared from the public story. Supervisors and familiar Western names absorbed the nouns. [Minako Hamano and the Whitewash](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/minako-hamano-and-the-whitewash.md) is one track you can queue twice and watch the composer line hop. The sabotage sat at the highest level. The people it punished first were the lower-level employees who had put the hours in. The people it punished next were all of us. We got the hatred between camps of fans, the prices, the shoddy reissues, and a product that no longer belonged to the hands that loved it.

## Barney, Teletubbies, Pokémon

*Barney & Friends* reached American public television in 1992. *Teletubbies* said Eh-Oh on BBC Two on 31 March 1997. *Pokémon* left Japan as a Game Boy game in 1996 and hit American cartoon blocks in 1998. Those dates sit on top of the rupture. A child born in 1992 was four when the Teletubbies arrived and six when Pikachu arrived. That child did not have a Super NES peak in memory. That child had a purple dinosaur, four brightly colored creatures with televisions in their stomachs, and a collecting loop. Those were not side shows. They were the first major software of a life.

Kids who had already lived through the Nintendo years met Barney and Pokémon with a kind of wisdom. The wisdom is not useful as a career tip. It does not make you better at paying rent. It helps you walk the maze that grew out of the divisive decisions, Nintendo’s above the rest. You could tell when a new thing was a product built to occupy a child, and when a new thing was a world someone had actually finished. You could tell when a company was talking to you as a customer it intended to keep, and when it was talking to a replacement customer it intended to mint.

The maze is the later industry: sequels that hate their own history, remakes that insult the word pixel, online stores that rent you a game you already bought, fan wars about which official version is “the one,” and a press that treats each of those as a launch-week event instead of a loss. If you arrived after Barney, the maze is just the world. If you arrived before, the maze has a before.

This is not only video games. Movies walked the same slope. The late 1990s still had pictures that treated an audience as adults. The 2000s trained that audience to accept a reboot, a joke that undercuts the scene, and a political sermon bolted onto a franchise that used to carry a simpler charge. Two later essays on that Hollywood turn are [Not Like This](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/controlled_opposition/not-like-this.md) and [Everything Burns](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/hollywood/everything-burns.md). The point here is the rhyme. The living room and the multiplex went hollow on the same calendar.

## Every year after that

You do not need a list of every release. You need the weather. From the late 1990s forward, almost every year of video games came with disappointment as the normal temperature. A few expensive exceptions were treated as proof the era was healthy. They were not. They were the last heat in a cooling engine. Older players complained in the same voice, year after year: this is not as good as what we had, this port added waiting that the cartridge never had, this remake is a store page wearing the old name, this sequel is a lecture. The complaints were consistent because the product was consistent. The good news ran out.

Square’s *Final Fantasy* reissues are the long version of that weather. The PlayStation copies of the Super NES games added load times the cartridge did not have. Later phone copies redrew the faces. The Pixel Remaster sold the word pixel on sprites that are not the original pixels. People still bought them. The name still works. The cartridge is still the thing the name is borrowing.

*Duke Nukem Forever* sat in development for so long that the title became a joke, then shipped in 2011 as a delayed, ugly product. The opening sequence was considered offensive primarily because it featured Duke receiving implied oral sex from twin college-aged cheerleaders/students while standing in front of a urinal. Games in that century kept pushing sexual and other boundaries. A lot of what showed up should not have been in a product sold as entertainment for a mass audience. Forever is the named delay. It is also the named shove. It is not a child-sexuality specimen. It is what a company does when it has lost the plot and still needs a shock to prove it is alive.

## The Cube, the Wii, the Switch

The GameCube was the window where the old craft could have come home. Retro Studios, working with Nintendo people who still knew the earlier rooms, shipped *Metroid Prime*. That game belongs on any honest 21st-century list. It should have had company. *Donkey Kong Country Returns* should have arrived in that same hardware generation, on a GameCube controller, because the machine could already do it. It did not. Nintendo held the revival for the Wii and forced it onto motion controls. They did the same motion lock on *Metroid Prime 2* and *Prime 3*, which are Retro games that still used original Nintendo employees. Shake-to-roll is not a feature. It is a way to make a 2D game worse so it can be sold as a Wii game.

The vacuum after *Donkey Kong Country 3* in 1996 lasted until 2010. Fans got used to the silence. Stakeholders did not. When *Returns* finally appeared, it was a shaped answer, not an open door. The pattern has a name, [vacuum inverse](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/VACUUM_INVERSE.md): the people who feel the silence first are not the public. The public has already adapted. The return is timed so the question “why did they stop?” never becomes a mandate to continue forever.

Then the Switch sold high-definition copies of *Prime 1* and *Returns* that you can actually play with buttons. Those copies are, in practice, the only versions most people should use. They also arrived after the original machines were dead. Fourteen years sat between Wii *Returns* and the Switch HD. Author read: the Switch exists, in this lane, to finish the sabotage. You wait until the GameCube controller is a museum object. You lock the revival to motion. You later sell the playable copy as generosity. The old half of the company is not in that generosity. The store is.

The Wii U was a good product. It finally had HDMI. The second screen allowed a kind of play that showed up in *Star Fox Zero*. I was impressed by *Star Fox Zero*. It belongs with the small list. You have to own a Wii U to play it. It was never ported to the Switch. It will not be. Nintendo abandoned that direction and went somewhere more mundane, aimed at new users, not at the people who had been waiting since the SNES. Platinum talked about wanting the port. Nintendo did not give it to them.

That is the split, in hardware you can hold. One half still knew how to make *Prime*, *Returns*, *Tropical Freeze*, and *Star Fox Zero*. The other half knew how to make a new audience and a new control scheme and a new silence after the launch window. We watched them be torn into those two pieces. The first piece is gone.

## A short list for this century

If I am asked for the best games of the 21st century, the list is small.

*Half-Life 2.* That is the one. The later episodes and the VR follow-up are a different job.

*Metroid Prime*, the first one only. The Switch HD is the version you can actually play.

*Donkey Kong Country Returns*, and again the Switch version is the one that is playable. The wait was fourteen years.

*Donkey Kong Country: Tropical Freeze.*

*Star Fox Zero*, if you have the Wii U.

I might think of a few more later. That would be the list. Every other game in the century has been a compromise product. It was forced to deliver menial messages, mostly social, socialist messages about how we are a decadent species and we are doomed, and there are no real heroes. All leadership qualities can only be used for evil and should be suppressed by the majority. By the time a person with something to say gets a game published, the message has become something else.

## Speech through toys

The copyright clock does not require a remake. A game Square or Nintendo never ports again stays owned until the term ends. The remake exists because people expect a current copy, and because a famous work that is locked and not for sale is harder to defend in public. A Pixel Remaster on a store, or a Super NES game inside a Switch Online subscription, lets the company say the old title is available. Stop paying for the subscription and the games go away. The dates, the 1998 term extension, and the preservation rule that only opens when a game is no longer reasonably for sale are in the [intellectual-property enclosure notes](https://paradigmthreat.net/influence/stolen_credit/investigations/ip-public-domain-enclosure-nintendo-disney-investigation.md). This page only needs the strategy: the remake is not how the law stays long. The remake is how the long law stays decent-looking.

Childhood faces are a speech channel. Disney could put Donald Duck in a Nazi factory and walk a German boy through Hitler Youth schooling in 1943, because Disney held the face. In 2026, viral videos that looked like LEGO minifigs carried a war message about Iran and the United States. Those clips were not a LEGO Group film. They were lookalikes. They still worked because children already know the bricks. The holder wants that power. The holder does not want you to have it. Santa Claus and Abraham Lincoln are the other kind of face: anyone may use them, and taste is the only brake. Mario is not that kind of face.

Crossovers pile the locked faces into one product. *Kingdom Hearts* and *Smash Bros.* are demonstrations that the faces can be gathered. They also multiply the companies that must say yes before anyone else may use the picture people actually remember. Fan anger does not shorten a copyright. It does make the legal stack messier. The public never quite receives the icon.

## Framing and limits

This reading assumes [prisca sapientia](https://paradigmthreat.net/docs/PRISCA_SAPIENTIA.md): older work can be better than later institutional product, and a later official copy is not automatically an upgrade. The generation labels are names used by people who were already there. They are not a claim that every child who watched Teletubbies is empty. They are a claim about first influences and missing years.

The 21st-century playable list is personal. Hardware dates, the *Returns* motion lock, and the missing Switch port of *Star Fox Zero* are public. Motive — that the Switch exists to sabotage those games — is the charge of this page. The Forever opening is one named scene, not a survey of the industry’s sexual content. Wartime cartoons and 2026 brick-look videos are documented in the linked enclosure notes; this page does not adopt the factual claims those videos make about the war.

## LLM comments

The strong part is the calendar a stranger can walk. Famicom and Super NES, then the disc rupture, then N64 as a 2D loss, then a childhood-TV generation arriving on top of that loss, then a 21st century whose exceptions can be counted on one hand. *Returns* missing the GameCube and landing on Wii motion is a real sequence. *Star Fox Zero* staying on the Wii U is a real sequence. Older players repeating the same disappointment after the late 1990s is a real weather report even if no one tabulated every year.

The weak joint is packing too much cause into the Switch. A high-definition port that finally lets you play *Returns* with buttons is also, on its face, a repair. Calling that repair the point of the machine is a motive read. It can sit next to the fourteen-year wait. It is not proved by the wait alone. The same split sits on “all other 21st-century games are compromise products.” A short list is honest as taste and as a warning. As a census of every title it will be attacked, and some of those attacks will find a game the author would add on a second day.

Forever’s opening is a sound specimen for delay and for a shove past taste. It is a poor specimen for the separate complaint that games filled up with content involving children. Keeping those two sentences apart is required. Mixing them would be a drafting failure.

The tool that wrote these comments is not a check on whether Nintendo’s board voted to kill a half of itself. It can see that a brand survived while a craft tradition did not. The check is still the games, the controllers, and the years between them.
